**Neuroplasticity** is the brain's ability to reorganize itself by forming new neural connections, strengthening existing ones, and removing unused ones in response to experience, learning, injury, or environmental demands. It demonstrates that the brain is not a fixed organ but changes dynamically across the lifespan.

---

## Key Processes

| Process | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Dendritic branching** | When a neuron is repeatedly stimulated, its dendrites grow in number and complexity, forming stronger connections with other neurons. Practice and learning drive this process. |
| **Synaptic pruning** | Under-stimulated synaptic connections are eliminated to increase efficiency. The brain removes connections that are no longer needed: "use it or lose it." |
| **Neurogenesis** | The creation of new neurons, primarily in the hippocampus. Challenges the earlier assumption that the adult brain cannot generate new cells. |
| **Cortical remapping** | A brain region reallocates its function following injury or sustained new demands. One area takes over the role previously performed by a damaged or disused region. |

---

## How Neuroplasticity Works

Neural networks develop through the making and breaking of **synaptic connections**. When we learn something, neurons that fire together repeatedly wire together (Hebb's rule): dendrites grow and connect, creating a new memory trace. When a connection is left unstimulated, it is pruned for efficiency.

This means neuroplasticity operates in **two directions**:
- **Growth**: new experience and learning produce new synaptic connections and grey matter
- **Pruning**: disuse causes those connections to weaken and eventually be eliminated

Both directions were demonstrated in the same study by Draganski (2004): grey matter increased after three months of juggling training, then partially decreased after training stopped.

---

## Types of Neuroplasticity

**Experience-dependent plasticity:** The brain changes in response to repeated experience or skill learning. This is demonstrated by Maguire (2000) in taxi drivers and Draganski (2004) in jugglers.

**Compensatory plasticity:** Following brain injury, surrounding regions or the opposite hemisphere take over functions previously handled by the damaged area. This is demonstrated by cortical remapping in stroke and lesion patients.

**Developmental plasticity:** The brain undergoes rapid synaptic growth and pruning during childhood and adolescence as it adapts to the environment it develops in.

## Linked Studies

### Maguire et al. (2000) - Taxi Drivers
London taxi drivers who had memorized 25,000 streets showed significantly greater **posterior hippocampal grey matter volume** than non-taxi drivers on MRI. The volume correlated positively with years of taxi driving experience. Supports experience-dependent structural change in the adult brain.

### Draganski et al. (2004) - Juggling
Participants who learned to juggle over three months showed increased grey matter density in the **mid-temporal area (V5/MT)** and **left posterior intraparietal sulcus**. After a three-month rest period, grey matter partially decreased, demonstrating both growth (plasticity) and pruning in the same study.

### Rosenzweig, Bennett & Diamond (1972) - Rat Study
Rats raised in enriched environments (toys, social interaction) developed **greater cortical thickness, more synaptic connections, and higher acetylcholinesterase activity** than rats in impoverished environments. Foundational evidence that environmental stimulation drives structural brain change.

### Tierney et al. - Case Study
A 37-year-old male suffered a lesion to **Broca's area** in the frontal lobe, impairing speech production. Over time, adjacent areas reorganized to compensate, demonstrating **cortical remapping** following injury.

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Convergent evidence**: human MRI studies (Maguire, Draganski) and animal studies (Rosenzweig) all support structural brain change through experience | **Causality**: Maguire's quasi-experimental design cannot confirm that navigation caused hippocampal growth; pre-existing differences in hippocampal size may attract people to become taxi drivers |
| **Objective measures** (MRI, grey matter density, cortical thickness) reduce self-report bias | **Generalisability**: findings from specific skills (juggling, navigation) may not apply to all types of learning |
| Draganski (2004) demonstrates **both plasticity and pruning** in the same study using a controlled experimental design | **Animal research** (Rosenzweig): enriched lab environments may not map directly onto human cognitive environments |
| Neuroplasticity has major **practical applications**: rehabilitation after stroke, educational practice, cognitive training | Individual differences in baseline neuroplasticity are not fully accounted for |
| Challenges the once-dominant view that the adult brain is fixed and cannot change | The mechanisms underlying structural change (exactly how experience produces grey matter increase) are not fully understood |

> **Key Concept - Use It or Lose It:**
> *Neuroplasticity is bidirectional. The brain grows new connections in response to learning and sustained stimulation, and prunes connections that fall into disuse. Draganski (2004) is the most elegant demonstration of this: both growth and pruning were observed in the same participants within the same six-month study. This has direct implications for education, rehabilitation, and understanding how both enrichment and deprivation shape the developing brain.*
