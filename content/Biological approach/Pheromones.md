## Definition

A **pheromone** is a chemical substance produced and released into the environment by an animal that affects the behaviour or physiology of others of **the same species**. The role of pheromones in human behaviour is controversial: no human pheromone has been conclusively identified to date.

---

## Types of Pheromones

**Primer pheromones** cause slow, long-term physiological changes, such as hormonal or reproductive effects. In humans, there is stronger evidence for primer pheromones than for signalling pheromones.

**Signalling pheromones** produce rapid behavioural effects, such as mating responses.

Some researchers also propose a third category: **modulator pheromones**, which affect mood or mental state (e.g. alarm signals in sweat).

---

## Animal Evidence

**Karlson and Luscher (1959)** found that a single molecule of bombykol released by a female silk moth could attract a male from over 2 km away. This is an example of a signalling pheromone.

**[[Bruce (1959)]]** found that if a recently impregnated female mouse was placed with an unfamiliar male, the miscarriage rate rose dramatically to 41%. This became known as the **Bruce Effect**.
- The IV was the identity of the male the female was exposed to after mating (same male vs. unfamiliar male).
- In follow-up experiments, removing the olfactory bulbs of the female eliminated the effect, implicating olfactory (smell) input.
- **Interpretation**: Bruce argued pheromones from the unfamiliar male, detected via the VNO, triggered neuroendocrine changes blocking implantation. Evolutionarily, this allows the female to become fertile and mate with the dominant male.
- **Evaluation**: The effect may instead reflect a **non-specific stress response** to a novel male (cortisol disrupting implantation), not a specific pheromonal signal. Until the specific chemical compound is isolated, the pheromone explanation remains speculative. The study also uses animals; direct transfer to humans is not warranted.

---

## The VNO Problem

Most animals detect pheromones via the **vomeronasal organ (VNO)**, an auxiliary olfactory organ that projects to the accessory olfactory bulb and then to the amygdala and hypothalamus. It expresses receptor families V1R, V2R, and FPR, distinct from the main olfactory system.

In humans, the VNO is controversial:
- Most studies agree the organ **regresses during foetal development**.
- Many genes essential for VNO function in animals are **non-functional in humans**.
- **Trotier et al. (2000)** used endoscopy (2,031 adults) and CT scans (7 adults) and concluded that while pits resembling a VNO exist, it is a **structural remnant** with no receptor neurons and does not function as a sensory organ.

However, the absence of a functional VNO does not definitively rule out pheromone effects in humans. Wyatt notes that in some animals, pheromones are also detected by the **main olfactory system**, so a VNO is not strictly necessary.

## Human Evidence

### For pheromone-like effects:

**[[McClintock (1971)]]** reported menstrual cycle synchronisation in women living in close proximity, suggesting chemical signalling influences reproductive timing (primer pheromone candidate).
- **Challenged by Yang & Schank (2006)**, who studied 186 Chinese women and concluded that the appearance of synchrony was a product of **invalid statistical analysis**, not a real effect.

**[[Zhou et al (2014) - Boar AND & EST]]** found that androstadienone (AND) and estratetraenol (EST) systematically biased perception of biological sex in a point-light walker task, in a sexuality-specific direction. Supports a pheromone-like function.
- **Challenged by Hare et al. (2017)**, who failed to replicate the findings. Also, the doses used were significantly higher than natural human secretion levels, limiting ecological validity.

**Stern & McClintock (1998)** found that exposure to compounds from the armpit secretions of women at different stages of their menstrual cycle could shift the timing of the recipients' ovulation, consistent with primer pheromone action.

**Huoviala & Rantala (2013)** found that AND increased cooperative behaviour between males in decision-making tasks, and that salivary testosterone correlated positively with cooperation.

**Doucet et al. (2009)** found evidence that areolar gland secretions from lactating mothers may facilitate bonding and initiation of breastfeeding in newborns, a candidate primer pheromone in humans.

### Against pheromone-like effects:

- No human pheromone has been **isolated, named, or conclusively identified**.
- The **Wedekind (1995)** sweaty T-shirt study shows women prefer the scent of men with dissimilar MHC immune genes, but this is not pheromone action: it operates at the **individual level**, not the species level, and involves a complex odour print (too many compounds to reduce to a single pheromone).
- Human chemical communication may reflect **individual odour prints** (influenced by diet, health, genetics, bacteria) rather than species-level pheromone signals.
- The **confounding variable of bacteria** on the skin metabolises secretions, altering scent in ways that are difficult to control.
- **Culture** is also a confound: learned responses to scent may explain findings attributed to pheromones.

## IB Exam Framing

The IB does not require students to argue that human pheromones exist. The expected answer is a **balanced discussion**: there is increasing evidence that chemicals may influence human behaviour in pheromone-like ways, but no human pheromone has been conclusively identified. The existence of the VNO problem, failed replications, and the inability to isolate specific compounds all limit the conclusions that can be drawn.

> Note: Wedekind (1995) is **not** a clean example of pheromones at work. It shows odour-based mate preference, but the odour print involved is too chemically complex to constitute a pheromone, and no specific compound has been identified.
