Neurotransmission is the biological process by which **neurons communicate with each other** using chemical messengers called **neurotransmitters**. It is the foundation of almost all behaviour, cognition, and emotion. Understanding it is essential for the Biological Approach in IB Psychology.

---

## The Two Messaging Systems

The body uses two systems to send internal messages:

| System | Speed | Medium | Pathway | Range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Endocrine system** | Slow | Hormones | Bloodstream | Distal (whole body) |
| **Nervous system** | Fast | Electrochemical signals | Neurons and synapses | Local and central |

The nervous system divides into:
- **Central Nervous System (CNS)**: brain, brainstem, and spinal cord
- **Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)**: motor and sensory neurons connecting body to brain

---

## Structure of a Neuron
![[Synapse Diagram and structure of neuron.png]]

Neurons are the basic unit of the nervous system. They transmit signals both electrically (within the cell) and chemically (between cells).

| Part | Function |
|---|---|
| **Cell body (soma)** | Contains the nucleus; processes incoming signals |
| **Dendrites** | Branch-like extensions that receive signals from other neurons |
| **Axon** | Long fibre that carries the electrical signal away from the cell body |
| **Myelin sheath** | Fatty coating that insulates the axon and speeds up signal transmission |
| **Terminal buttons** | Tips of the axon that release neurotransmitters into the synapse |
| **Synapse** | The gap between two neurons where chemical communication occurs |

---

## How Neurotransmission Works

1. An **electrical impulse** (action potential) travels down the axon to the terminal buttons.
2. The terminal buttons release **neurotransmitters** into the **synaptic gap**.
3. Neurotransmitters bind to **receptor sites** on the dendrites of the next (post-synaptic) neuron.
4. The signal is either **excitatory** (making the next neuron more likely to fire) or **inhibitory** (making it less likely to fire).
5. Leftover neurotransmitters are broken down by enzymes or reabsorbed by the sending neuron through **reuptake**.

> If neurotransmitters are **blocked, depleted, or altered**, the message changes, affecting behaviour, mood, cognition, or physical function.

## Key Neurotransmitters

### Acetylcholine (ACh)

**Primary role:** Memory, learning, muscle movement, and attention.

- Found in high concentrations in the **hippocampus**, the brain region central to memory encoding.
- Low ACh activity is associated with **Alzheimer's disease** (significant hippocampal ACh depletion).
- **Agonists** (increase ACh activity): Physostigmine (blocks the enzyme that breaks it down)
- **Antagonists** (block ACh activity): Scopolamine (blocks muscarinic receptors)

**Relevant studies:**
- [[Martinez & Kesner]]: Pharmacological manipulation of ACh in rats confirmed a causal role in spatial memory.
- [[Rogers and Kesner]]: Direct hippocampal ACh blockade impaired spatial memory encoding specifically.
- [[Antonova et al (2011)]]: Human fMRI study showed scopolamine reduces hippocampal activation during spatial navigation.

---

### Noradrenaline (Norepinephrine)

**Primary role:** Alertness, arousal, attention, and mood regulation. Also involved in the stress response (fight-or-flight).

- Produced mainly in the **locus coeruleus** (brainstem region).
- **Low noradrenaline** is associated with **depression** and reduced motivation.
- **High noradrenaline** increases alertness and heart rate (part of sympathetic nervous system activation).
- **SNRIs** (serotonin-noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors) are a class of antidepressants that work by increasing noradrenaline and serotonin at synapses.

**Relevant study:**
- [[Jarowsky et al]]: Pharmacologically reducing noradrenaline in healthy participants induced significant depressive symptoms, providing direct causal evidence for the catecholamine hypothesis.

---

### Dopamine

**Primary role:** Reward, motivation, pleasure, and movement.

- Central to the **mesolimbic reward pathway** (nucleus accumbens, VTA).
- **Excess dopamine** in certain brain regions is associated with **schizophrenia** (dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia).
- **Depleted dopamine** in the motor regions (substantia nigra) causes **Parkinson's disease**.
- Plays a key role in romantic love and addiction (see [[Fisher et al 2005]]).

---

### Serotonin

**Primary role:** Mood regulation, sleep, appetite, and emotional stability.

- Mostly produced in the **raphe nuclei** (brainstem).
- **Low serotonin** is associated with **depression, anxiety, and OCD**.
- **SSRIs** (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, e.g., fluoxetine/Prozac) prevent serotonin from being reabsorbed, increasing its availability at synapses.
- Marazziti et al. (1999) found serotonin levels in new romantic partners resembled those found in OCD patients, suggesting obsessive early-love thinking has a neurochemical basis.

## Summary Table: Neurotransmitters & Behaviour

| Neurotransmitter | Associated With | Deficit Linked To | Excess Linked To |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acetylcholine (ACh)** | Memory, learning, muscle control | Memory impairment, Alzheimer's | Muscle overstimulation |
| **Noradrenaline** | Alertness, arousal, stress response | Depression, fatigue | Anxiety, high blood pressure |
| **Dopamine** | Reward, motivation, movement | Parkinson's, low motivation | Schizophrenia |
| **Serotonin** | Mood, sleep, appetite | Depression, anxiety, OCD | Serotonin syndrome (rare) |

## Research Methods in Neurotransmission Research

Researchers use several methods to study neurotransmitters:

| Method | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Pharmacological manipulation** | Administer drugs that act as agonists or antagonists to increase or block NT activity | Martinez & Kesner (physostigmine/scopolamine in rats) |
| **Brain imaging (fMRI, PET)** | Measure brain activity during tasks; PET can track glucose metabolism; fMRI measures blood oxygenation | Antonova et al. (fMRI + scopolamine); Raine et al. (PET scans) |
| **Animal studies** | Invasive manipulation of NT levels or receptor sites; allows direct causal testing | Rogers & Kesner (hippocampal ACh blockade in rats) |
| **Double-blind experiments** | Administer NT-altering drugs to human participants under controlled conditions | Jarowsky et al. (noradrenaline depletion) |

| Study | Neurotransmitter | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|

## IB Exam Relevance

**Common SAQ prompts:**
- *"Explain the role of neurotransmitters in human behaviour."*
- *"Describe one study related to neurotransmission."*
- *"Explain how one neurotransmitter influences behaviour."*

**Common ERQ prompts:**
- *"Discuss the relationship between neurotransmission and behaviour."*
- *"Evaluate research into the role of neurotransmitters in one behaviour."*
- *"To what extent do biological factors explain [depression / aggression / memory]?"*

**For SAQs:** Pick one neurotransmitter (ACh or noradrenaline), define it, and describe one study fully in APPR format.

**For ERQs:** Use two studies (e.g., Martinez & Kesner + Antonova for ACh; or Jarowsky for noradrenaline), evaluate their methods individually, then add holistic CT on issues like animal research generalizability, ethical concerns with drug studies, or reductionism.

---

> **Key Concept - Neurotransmission & Behaviour:**
> *Behaviour is not simply the product of conscious choice but of electrochemical communication between billions of neurons. When neurotransmitters are altered, through drugs, disease, or environmental factors, behaviour changes in predictable and measurable ways. This is why neurotransmission is foundational to the biological explanation of behaviour: it provides a mechanistic, falsifiable account of how the brain produces thought, emotion, and action.*
