## Introduction

Cognitive psychologists have developed multiple models to explain human cognitive processes. The **dual processing model (Kahneman, 2011)** explains human cognition through two systems. **System 1** is fast but prone to errors and **System 2** is slower but more accurate.

System 2 handles consciously monitored rational and logical reasoning for complex decisions. It is slower but more reliable than its counterpart; System 1 on the other hand, is responsible for "quick thinking" (automatic and intuitive decision-making). This system relies on the activation of pre-existing knowledge and mental shortcuts (heuristics) to optimise quick but less accurate responses **(Stanovich and West, 2000)**.

Its reliance on heuristics is problematic as it can lead to systematic errors in judgment. Otherwise known as cognitive biases, these take place as humans are cognitive misers, and tend to rely on mental shortcuts as argued by **Fiske & Taylor (1991)**. Heuristics have been described as _"simple rules of thumb that help us navigate complex decisions but can sometimes lead to predictable errors"_ **(Tversky & Kahneman, 1974)**.

One such bias is the **Anchoring effect**, where individuals base decisions on the first piece of information encountered (known as the 'Anchor'). For instance, in Black Friday sales, displaying original prices alongside discounts can anchor consumer perception, leading to consumers perceiving discounts as more significant, hence increasing their willingness to spend. **Strack & Mussweiler (1997)** argued that _"the anchoring effect occurs when there is only general knowledge about the value of something and little information besides the anchor value is provided."_

**Strack & Mussweiler (1997)** investigated how anchoring bias influences decision-making. They used an opportunity sample of 69 German undergraduates. Participants were tasked with a "general knowledge questionnaire" and first exposed to an implausible anchor, such as: _"Did Mahatma Gandhi die before or after the age of 140?"_ In one condition, participants received a high anchor, while the other received a low anchor. Subsequently, participants were asked to provide an estimate of how old he was when he died. The study found that even absurd anchors influenced participant responses. Surprisingly, the low anchor had a greater influence than the high anchor.

Anchoring bias occurs because individuals rely on System 1 thinking, prioritizing speed over accuracy. When making university or career-related decisions, young students may rely on initial information rather than critically evaluating and investigating their options.

While Strack & Mussweiler (1997) examined anchoring influence in general knowledge tasks, this study explores whether anchoring bias extends to **real-world decision-making**. Specifically, our study aims to investigate whether **the order of positive and negative information** influences 15-year-old students' judgment and perceptions of a university. Understanding how cognitive biases influence such judgements could inform strategies that promote more analytical and rational thinking among students.

### Hypotheses

**H₀:** The order in which positive and negative information about a university is presented **will have no significant influence** on 15-year-old, northern Spain high school students' perception of the university, as measured by their Likert scale rating (1–10) of willingness to attend. Students exposed to positive information first are predicted to show **no significant difference** in their median willingness-to-attend rating, compared to those exposed to negative information first (Negative Anchor).

**H₁:** The order in which positive and negative information about a university is presented **will significantly influence** 15-year-old, northern Spain high school students' perception of the university, as measured by their Likert scale rating (1–10) of willingness to attend that university. Students who are exposed to **positive information first** are predicted to report a **higher median** willingness-to-attend rating and those who are exposed to **negative information first (Negative Anchor)** are predicted to report a **lower median** willingness-to-attend.

## Exploration

This experiment used an **independent sample design**; participants were randomly assigned to either condition (A or B). Each participant received only one version of the review, preserving **credibility** and **experimental integrity**. This design avoided **practice or fatigue effects** and minimised **demand characteristics**, as exposure to both versions might have revealed the aim, compromising **internal validity**.

In **Condition A (treatment group)**, negative aspects were presented first, serving as an anchor. In **Condition B (control group)**, negative information was presented later to eliminate the anchoring effect.

### Participants

The **target population** was a group of **15–16-year-old students from a private bilingual school in northern Spain, fluent in English and unfamiliar with IB psychology.** An **opportunity sample** of **37 students**, male and female (18 in condition A, 19 in condition B), was recruited via Year 11 homeroom teachers. Participants were chosen for being:

- Fluent in English (avoiding language bias)
- Unfamiliar with IB psychology and anchoring bias
- At an age where educational decision-making becomes more relevant

This homogeneity **limits generalizability**, as cognitive processes may differ with age or cultural backgrounds.

### Allocation

**Random allocation** of participants into experimental conditions was achieved using a **random number generator**, spreading variability across the two conditions and ensuring all participants had an equal chance of assignment.

### Controls

To **minimize rehearsal effects** and encourage **intuitive System 1 thinking**, participants were given a **3-minute time constraint**. Additionally, participants worked **individually and in silence** to prevent social desirability biases such as conformity. Standardized instructions and review materials were used for both conditions.

### Ethical Considerations

- **Parental consent** obtained for participants under 16 (see Appendix 5)
- Participants were **briefed** (Appendix 1) on their right to withdraw from the experiment or withdraw their data at any moment
- Participants were instructed to keep responses **anonymous** by not writing their names
- All participants were **debriefed** (Appendix 3) after the study, eliminating any deception and undoing any stress or harm caused

### Materials

- **Printed university reviews:** Prevented demand characteristics and participants comparing versions or guessing the aim of the study.
- **Printed Likert scale rating paper:** Ensured anonymity, avoided conformity effects and reduced peer influence. (Printing was preferred to avoid internet issues or distractions.)
- **Consent forms:** Legal requirement for underage participants.
- **Random Number Generator:** Ensured random allocation of participants into conditions and preserved anonymity.

## Analysis

_Raw data shown in Appendix 6._

Since the data was collected using Likert scales, which have ordered categories but the distance between categories is unknown or subjective, it is considered **ordinal**. The **median** was chosen as the measure of central tendency. The variability was measured using **Interquartile Range (IQR)**, used to eliminate outliers that may distort interpretation.

_Figure 1: Calculated data — Median and IQR_

||Condition A (Negative anchor at beginning)|Condition B (Negative anchor last)|
|---|---|---|
|**Median rating**|5.5|7.0|
|**IQR**|4.0|1.5|

Students from Condition A, presented with the negative information first, reported a **lower median willingness** to attend the university (5.5) compared to students from Condition B, which received the negative information at the end (7.0). This suggests that the order in which information is presented does have a moderate influence (difference of 1.5 points on the Likert scale) over students' perception, supporting the research hypothesis.

The box and whiskers plot (Appendix 7) provides further insight:

- Condition A has a larger IQR (4.0), indicating greater variability when negative information was presented first.
- Condition B has a smaller IQR (1.5), suggesting greater consistency in responses.
- Condition A shows a wider spread of responses ranging from 2 to 9, whereas Condition B ranged from 3 to 8, suggesting greater response polarization in Condition A.
- An outlier in Condition B was noted, where one participant rated significantly lower than the group.

### Statistical Significance — Mann Whitney U-Test

Given the reduced sample size (N=37), independent sample design, and the ordinal nature of the data, a **Mann Whitney U-Test** was applied to assess statistical significance. The test was **one-tailed** with a **significance level of 0.05** (p ≤ 0.05 indicating statistical significance).

Results (Appendix 8) **support** the research hypothesis:

|Statistic|Value|
|---|---|
|**U-value**|87.5 (lower than critical value of 116 at p < 0.05)|
|**Z-score**|2.52211|
|**P-value**|0.00587|

The **P-value (0.00587)** falls within the 0.05 significance level. If the null hypothesis were true, there would be a 0.587% probability of obtaining such extreme results. Thus, we **confidently reject the null hypothesis**.

Additionally, the mean rank difference (**14.36 in Condition A vs. 23.39 in Condition B**) reinforces the conclusion that information order influences decision-making. Students in Condition B, where negative information was presented last, had significantly higher willingness-to-attend ratings on average.

## Evaluation

Our findings align with **Strack and Mussweiler (1997)**, which demonstrated that individuals rely on an initial reference point when making judgements. In our study, Condition A (negative anchor first) participants reported a median willingness-to-attend rating of **5.5**, whereas those in Condition B (negative anchor last) had a median rating of **7.0**.

**The Dual Processing Model (Kahneman, 2011)** provides an explanation. Under time pressure and with limited context, participants likely relied on **System 1** (fast, automatic, heuristic-based) rather than **System 2** (slower, effortful, analytical). The first information presented (anchor) acted as a heuristic shortcut, explaining why those exposed to negative information first gave lower ratings.

### Strengths

- **Independent sample design** reduced demand characteristics and practice effects, since participants were only exposed to one version of the review.
- Both conditions received identical content, differing only in the order of positive and negative information, effectively **isolating the independent variable**.
- Use of **standardized instructions and materials** ensured consistency across conditions and minimized experimenter bias.

### Limitations and Future Modifications

**Limitation 1: Participant variability** Differences in prior attitudes, familiarity with decision-making or susceptibility to cognitive biases may have influenced responses.

> **Modification:** Pre-screen participants for similar levels of awareness or decision-making tendencies using a brief questionnaire.

**Limitation 2: Generalizability** The target population of 15-year-old bilingual private school students in northern Spain limits generalizability, as the sample lacks diversity in socioeconomic and cultural experiences. However, their age and inexperience could be a strength as it likely increased reliance on **System 1 thinking**, enhancing the anchoring effect.

**Limitation 3: Ecological validity** The controlled classroom setting may limit ecological validity.

> **Modification:** Replicate the study in a more naturalistic setting, such as during a university fair.

**Limitation 4: Attention span** Some participants may have found the texts too long, leading to skim reading and shallow processing of the anchor information.

> **Modification:** Simplify and shorten the review texts while retaining key information, and include embedded attention checks to identify disengaged participants.

### Conclusion

Our findings are concurrent with those of **Strack and Mussweiler (1997)**, showing consistency with the **Dual Process Model** and **Tversky and Kahneman's (1974)** theory on cognitive biases and heuristics. The order in which information was presented showed a significant influence on the Likert scale score: those presented with negative information first showed a lower median score than those presented with positive information at the beginning.

Therefore, we **reject the null hypothesis** and **conclude** anchoring bias affects judgment and decision-making in **15-year-old bilingual high school students in northern Spain with no prior knowledge of IB psychology**.
