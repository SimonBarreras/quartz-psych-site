# correlational studies
- Quantitative approach to studying behaviour 
- Correlational studies should have **no manipulation** from researcher.
- Must yield **statistical results** 
- Most common data collection through **Questionnaires** and **surveys** or **Psychometric tests** (e.g. IQ tests)
- Regression Analysis
- Inductive content analysis (no statistical measurement but interpreting anf drawing conclusions) (**Does not** qualify as "correlational study" although it might show correlation between variables).

## Question 1 — Research Methods & Sampling (9 marks)

> [!warning] Key Rule
> **Do not use bullet points** for Q1. Write in full sentences. Each question is worth 3 marks: **1 mark for correct identification + 2 marks for two correct characteristics**.

### 1a — Identify the research method + outline two characteristics (3 marks)

Identify the method used in the stimulus, then describe two of its defining characteristics in your own words. Always link to the stimulus where possible.

#### Research Methods to Know

| Method | Key Characteristics | Qualitative or Quantitative? |
|---|---|---|
| **Experiment (lab)** | Controlled environment; manipulation of IV; measurement of DV; random allocation possible | Quantitative |
| **Experiment (field)** | Natural setting; manipulation of IV; higher ecological validity; less control | Quantitative |
| **Observation (naturalistic)** | Behaviour observed in natural setting; no manipulation; high ecological validity; risk of observer bias | Qualitative / Quantitative |
| **Observation (participant)** | Researcher joins the group being studied; rich insider data; risk of researcher bias | Qualitative |
| **Structured interview** | Pre-set closed questions; standardised; allows comparison; less depth | Quantitative |
| **Semi-structured interview** | Pre-set topics but open-ended questions; conversational; allows elaboration; flexible | Qualitative |
| **Unstructured interview** | No set questions; free-flowing; very rich data; hard to replicate | Qualitative |
| **Focus group** | Group discussion facilitated by researcher; explores shared views; social dynamics may affect responses | Qualitative |
| **Survey / Questionnaire** | Written self-report; large samples possible; standardised; risk of social desirability bias | Quantitative |
| **Case study** | In-depth study of one individual or group; rich, detailed data; not generalisable | Qualitative |
| **Correlational study** | Measures relationship between two variables; no manipulation; cannot establish cause and effect | Quantitative |

---

### 1b — Describe the sampling method used (3 marks)

Same structure: **1 mark for identifying** the correct method + **2 marks for two characteristics**. Connect to the stimulus (e.g. mention the sample size or group described).

#### Sampling Methods to Know

| Method | How it works | Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Opportunity / Convenience** | Whoever is available at the time | Easy and quick | Likely biased; not representative |
| **Volunteer / Self-selected** | Participants respond to an advert or request | Easy to recruit; willing participants | Self-selection bias; not representative |
| **Purposive** | Researcher deliberately selects participants with specific characteristics relevant to the study | Highly relevant sample | Researcher bias in selection; not generalisable |
| **Snowball** | Existing participants recruit further participants from their network | Useful for hard-to-reach groups | Chain bias; not representative |
| **Random** | Every member of the target population has an equal chance of being selected | Most representative method | Difficult and time-consuming in practice |
| **Stratified** | Population divided into subgroups; participants randomly selected from each in proportion | Representative of subgroups | Complex to organise |

---

### 1c — Suggest an additional (or alternative) research method + one reason (3 marks)

> [!tip] Strategy
> **Suggest an *additional* method rather than an *alternative* one** — it is easier to justify adding a method to complement the existing one than to argue for replacing it entirely.
> Always justify your choice by connecting it to what the original study **could not** do (e.g. small sample, lack of depth, low ecological validity).

**Example answer structure:**
*"An additional research method would be [method]. This is because [reason linked to the stimulus — e.g. the original study had only 12 participants, so a survey would allow a larger sample and enable statistical generalisation of the findings]."*

**Question:** *Describe the ethical considerations in reporting the results and explain ethical considerations that could be taken into account when applying the findings of the study.*

- **Part 1:** Describe how ethical considerations apply when **publishing and reporting** the results (e.g. anonymity, accurate representation of data).

### Ethical Considerations Reference List (CARDUD +)

> [!note] Aim to know at least 8–10 of these in depth

| Ethical Consideration | What it means | How to apply it |
|---|---|---|
| **Informed consent** | Participants must be told the nature of the study and agree before taking part | Provide a consent form; parental consent required for minors |
| **Right to withdraw** | Participants can leave at any time without penalty | Must be stated at the start; no pressure to continue |
| **Confidentiality** | Personal data must not be shared or identifiable | Use codes or pseudonyms; secure data storage |
| **Anonymity** | Participants cannot be identified from published results | Do not include names, locations, or identifying details in reports |
| **Protection from harm** | Participants must not experience undue physical or psychological distress | Debrief after study; screen for vulnerable participants |
| **Deception** | Misleading participants is generally avoided; if used, it must be justified | Must be debriefed fully afterwards; mild deception may be permissible |
| **Debriefing** | Participants are informed of the true aim after the study | Should restore any distress caused; explain why deception was necessary if used |
| **Accurate reporting** | Data must be presented honestly; no fabrication or selective omission | Negative results must also be reported; methods must be transparent |
| **Reputation of psychology** | Research must not bring the discipline into disrepute | Avoid sensationalist claims; be cautious with media communication |
| **Knock-on / societal effects** | Published findings can affect public groups beyond participants | Consider how findings might stigmatise groups or influence policy |
| **Funding / conflict of interest** | Funding sources may influence findings | Declare funding sources; ensure independence of analysis |
| **Cultural sensitivity** | Research must be respectful of cultural norms and values of participants | Adapt consent and procedure to cultural context |

### Variant 3a — Generalisation / Transferability

**Question:** *Discuss the possibility of generalising / transferring the findings of the study.*

> [!note] Key Distinction
> Whether you discuss **generalisation** (quantitative) or **transferability** (qualitative) depends on the type of study in the stimulus. Know both.

#### If the study is **quantitative** → discuss Generalisation
*"Refers to the extent to which findings can be applied to a broader population beyond the sample."*

- **Sample size** — larger samples increase likelihood of being representative
- **Sampling method** — random/stratified sampling → more representative; opportunity/snowball → less
- **Demographics** — is the sample WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic)? This limits generalisability
- **Ecological validity** — were conditions artificial (lab) or realistic? Low ecological validity limits generalisation to real life
- **Replication** — have findings been reproduced across different populations?

#### If the study is **qualitative** → discuss Transferability
*"Refers to the extent to which insights from the study could be relevant or meaningful in another context or population."*

- **Thick description** — detailed, rich accounts allow readers to judge whether findings fit their own context
- **Purposive sample** — if selected for specific characteristics, findings may transfer to others with those characteristics
- **Context-specificity** — qualitative findings are deeply tied to the specific setting; direct generalisation is not the goal
- **Theoretical generalisation** — findings may extend existing theory even without statistical generalisation

---

### Variant 3b — Credibility

**Question:** *Discuss how a researcher could ensure that the results of the study are credible.*

> [!note] This question usually only appears when the stimulus study uses **qualitative methods**.

*"Credibility refers to the extent to which the results of the study can be trusted — i.e. do they accurately represent the participants' reality?"*

| Strategy | What it involves |
|---|---|
| **Triangulation** | Using multiple methods, researchers, or data sources to cross-check findings |
| **Method triangulation** | Combining qualitative and quantitative methods (e.g. interview + survey) |
| **Researcher triangulation** | Multiple researchers independently analyse the same data and compare interpretations |
| **Data triangulation** | Collecting data at different times, locations, or from different participant groups |
| **Member checking** | Returning findings to participants to verify they accurately reflect their experience |
| **Prolonged engagement** | Spending extended time in the field to build trust and deepen understanding |
| **Peer debriefing** | Having colleagues scrutinise the researcher's methods and interpretations |
| **Reflexivity** | Researcher acknowledges and documents their own biases and how these may have shaped findings |
| **Audit trail** | Keeping a detailed record of all research decisions so the process can be independently reviewed |
| **Thick / rich description** | Providing detailed accounts that allow readers to assess credibility themselves |

---

### Variant 3c — Avoiding Bias

**Question:** *Discuss how a researcher could avoid bias in the study.*

*"Bias refers to any systematic distortion that prevents results from accurately reflecting reality."*

#### Sources of Bias

| Source | Type of Bias | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Researcher / Experimenter bias** | Researcher's expectations unconsciously influence results | Unconsciously asking leading questions in an interview |
| **Participant bias** | Participants modify behaviour because they are being observed | Social desirability — giving answers they think are expected |
| **Sampling bias** | Sample does not represent the target population | Using only university students (WEIRD sample) |
| **Confirmation bias** | Researcher interprets data to confirm existing beliefs | Ignoring contradictory qualitative data |
| **Demand characteristics** | Participants guess the aim and alter their behaviour | Guessing the hypothesis in an experiment |

#### Ways to Avoid Bias

| Strategy | How it reduces bias |
|---|---|
| **Single-blind procedure** | Participants do not know which condition they are in → reduces demand characteristics |
| **Double-blind procedure** | Neither participants nor researcher know condition allocation → eliminates experimenter bias |
| **Standardised procedures** | Same instructions and conditions for all participants → reduces researcher variability |
| **Reflexivity** | Researcher examines their own assumptions and documents them → reduces confirmation bias |
| **Triangulation** | Cross-checking findings across multiple methods/researchers → reduces single-source bias |
| **Random allocation** | Participants randomly assigned to conditions → reduces selection bias in experiments |
| **Appropriate sampling** | Using random or stratified sampling → reduces sample bias |
| **Pilot study** | Testing procedure in advance to identify and remove sources of bias before the main study |
| **Inductive Content Analysis (ICA)** | Bottom-up coding of qualitative data without pre-set categories → reduces confirmation bias in analysis |

> [!tip] ICA Note
> **Inductive Content Analysis** is a qualitative analysis technique used to analyse interviews, focus groups, and open-ended questionnaires. Categories emerge from the data rather than being imposed by the researcher. It does **not** produce statistical results and does **not** qualify as a correlational study, even if patterns emerge.

## Correlational Studies — Key Points

- A **quantitative** approach — must yield statistical results (e.g. correlation coefficient)
- **No manipulation** of variables by the researcher
- Most common data collection methods: surveys, questionnaires, psychometric tests (e.g. IQ tests)
- Analysis: Pearson's r, Spearman's rho, regression analysis
- **Cannot establish cause and effect** — only association between variables
- **Inductive Content Analysis does not qualify** as a correlational study — it produces no statistical measure of correlation

---

## Qualitative vs Quantitative — Quick Reference

| Feature | Qualitative | Quantitative |
|---|---|---|
| **Data type** | Words, themes, meanings | Numbers, statistics |
| **Goal** | Understand experience / behaviour in depth | Measure, compare, generalise |
| **Common methods** | Interviews, focus groups, case studies, naturalistic observation | Experiments, surveys, correlational studies |
| **Analysis** | Inductive Content Analysis, thematic analysis | Statistical analysis, regression |
| **Generalisation** | Transferability of insight | Statistical generalisation |
| **Strength** | Rich, contextual, participant-centred | Replicable, objective, comparable |
| **Limitation** | Hard to generalise; researcher bias risk | Lacks depth; may miss context |

### Common Mistakes to Avoid

- Using bullet points in Q1 — write in full sentences
- Only identifying the method/sampling without giving two characteristics
- Suggesting an *alternative* rather than an *additional* method in Q1c (harder to justify)
- Listing ethical considerations without explaining *how* they apply to the specific study
- Writing generic answers for Q3 — every point must connect back to the stimulus
- Confusing generalisation (quantitative) with transferability (qualitative)
- Treating ICA as a correlational method

- Correct identification + two clearly explained characteristics (Q1)

- Six distinct, well-explained ethical points in Q2
- Correct application of generalisation vs transferability based on study type (Q3a)
- Concrete strategies explained with their mechanism — not just named (Q3b/c)
