**Loftus and Palmer** conducted laboratory <u>Lab Experiments</u> investigating the effect of **leading questions** on eyewitness memory (EWM) through videos of traffic accidents, the participants were asked about the speed of the car in different ways. Demonstrating how post-event information can distort memory for a witnessed event. Key study for [[Reconstructive Memory]].  [[Reconstructive memory ERQ]]

[[Bartlett (1932) - War of Ghosts]]; humans try to find meaning in what they experience and if something seems unfamiliar they will try to fit the experience into existing schemas. 

[[Loftus & Pickrell (1995) - Lost in a mall]]; demonstrated that peoples's memory can be manipulated by post-event information and even implant false memories. 

> **Useful for:** *"Discuss research methods in the cognitive approach"* / *"Discuss the reliability of one cognitive process"* / *"Evaluate reconstructive memory"*

___

**AIM:** Investigate the effect of leading questions (specifically the verb used) on participants' memory estimates of a car crash's speed, and whether it can implant false memories of details that were not present.
>[!NOTE]
>Leading questions are defined as questions that either by form or content suggest to a witness which answer is desired. 

**Participants:** **45 American students**, divided into 5 groups of 9.

**Method:** <u>Lab experiment</u> with independent samples design. (ISD)

**Experiment 1:**
- Participants watched 7 film clips of car accidents. (5-30s)
- Each group was given a questionnaire including: *"About how fast were the cars going when they \_\_\_\_\_ into each other?"*
- The critical verb differed across groups: **contacted / hit / bumped / collided / smashed**

**Experiment 2:**
- A group of **150 participants**, randomly allocated, watched a multiple-collision clip. (4s)
- Participants where given a questionnaire, describing the accident in their own words and one of 3 questions (between others ofc):
	- 50 were asked about speed using **"smashed"** 
	- 50 we asked about speed using **"hit"**
	- 50 were not asked to estimate speed
- One week later, all were asked: *"Did you see any broken glass?"* (There was **no broken glass** in the film.)

**Results:**

*Experiment 1 - Speed Estimates:*

| Verb      | Mean Speed Estimate (mph) |
| --------- | ------------------------- |
| Smashed   | 40.8                      |
| Collided  | 39.3                      |
| Bumped    | 38.1                      |
| Hit       | 34.0                      |
| Contacted | 31.8                      |
The critical word consistently affected the estimations of speed given by the participants. Although studies like *Marshal (1969)*  have shown that it is particularly difficult to estimate the speed of a moving car. Thus, it may be argued that the estimates given are the result of response bias; influencing their answer but not their memory. 

This distortion of memory is based on reconstruction so that it is not the actual details of the accident that are remembered but what is in line with a cognitive schema of a car accident. 

*Experiment 2 - False Memory of Broken Glass:*

| Condition                   | Mean Speed Estimate (mph) | Reported seeing broken glass |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| "Smashed"                   | 10.46                     | **32%** said yes             |
| "Hit"                       | 8.00                      | **14%** said yes             |
| Control (no speed question) |                           | **12%** said yes             |
It was concluded that the wording of the critical question led to higher speed estimates and more "yes" answers. The word "smashed" suggests a more severe accident and therefore they were more likely to think that there was broken glass (compared to "hit").

**Conclusion:** Post-event information can alter memory for a witnessed event, through suggestive questions. The verb "smashed" not only activated the schema for higher speed estimates, but also led participants to falsely "remember" broken glass that was never there, demonstrating that memory is **reconstructive**, i.e., people tend to change details of an event when they try to remember it (Bartlett, 1932).

## Evaluation

| Strengths                                                                                   | Limitations                                                                                                                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **High internal validity**: controlled lab conditions; clear manipulation of one IV (verb)  | **Low ecological validity**: watching a film clip carries far less emotional weight than witnessing a real accident + it was done in a laboratory. |
| Experiment 2 establishes **cause-and-effect** between wording and false memory formation    | **Demand characteristics**: participants may have guessed the hypothesis                                                                           |
| Results are **replicable**; followed up by multiple studies (e.g., Loftus & Pickrell, 1995) | **Population bias**: American university students only; not representative + likely, young inexperienced drivers.                                  |
| Has major **real-world implications** for eyewitness testimony and legal proceedings        | Does not account for the role of **emotional arousal** on memory (cf. Yuille & Cutshall, 1986)                                                     |
| Two-experiment design strengthens conclusions                                               | Participants were tested on **film**, not a real event, which limits direct application to EWT                                                     |
|                                                                                             | Independent Samples Design does not account for participants variability                                                                           |

> **Key Concept - Reconstructive Memory:**
> *Memory is not a video recording but an active reconstruction influenced by prior knowledge (schemas), expectations, and post-event information. Loftus & Palmer (1974) showed that a single word in a question is enough to alter a memory and even create entirely false details. This has profound implications for the reliability of eyewitness testimony in criminal justice.*
