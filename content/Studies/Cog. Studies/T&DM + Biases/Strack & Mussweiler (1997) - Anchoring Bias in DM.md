#Study #cognitive [[Thinking & Decision Making]], [[Heuristics - Schema Theory]], [[Dual Processing Model (Systems 1 &2)]]
___

**Strack and Mussweiler** conducted a <u>Lab Experiment</u> investigating whether **anchoring bias** persists even when the anchor value is clearly implausible, testing the robustness of System 1 thinking under conditions where the anchor should logically be dismissed.
___

**Aim:** To test the influence of anchoring bias on decision-making and judgement, specifically examining whether implausible or outlandish anchors still distort numerical estimates.

**Participants:** **69 German undergraduates**, recruited via opportunity sampling from a university canteen at lunchtime. They were asked to take part in a general knowledge questionnaire.

**Method:** <u>Lab experiment</u> with independent groups design.
- Participants answered general knowledge questions on a **computer screen**. Each question had two components.
- Participants were **randomly allocated** to one of two conditions, receiving one of the following anchor questions about Mahatma Gandhi:
  - **Low anchor (implausible):** "Did Mahatma Gandhi die before or after the age of **9**?"
  - **High anchor (implausible):** "Did Mahatma Gandhi die before or after the age of **140**?"
- After answering, participants were asked to estimate: "How old was Mahatma Gandhi when he died?"
- The actual answer is **78 years old**.
- The study also included **plausible anchors** (64 and 79) and additional general knowledge questions covering topics such as the mean temperature in Antarctica in winter, the year of Da Vinci's birth, the length of a whale, the year of Einstein's first visit to the US, and the length of the Elbe River.

**Results:**

| Condition | Anchor | Mean Estimated Age |
|---|---|---|
| Low anchor (implausible) | 9 | **50.1** |
| High anchor (implausible) | 140 | **66.7** |
| Actual answer | n/a | 78 |

- Even though both anchors were clearly implausible, they still significantly influenced participants' estimates.
- The **low anchor (9) had a stronger pulling effect** than the high anchor (140), possibly because an age of 140 is perceived as truly impossible rather than merely implausible, reducing its anchoring power.
- The plausible anchor conditions showed a different pattern: the low plausible anchor (64) produced a mean estimate of **99.6**, while the high plausible anchor (79) produced a mean estimate of **67.9**.
- Across all questions, **high anchors consistently led to higher estimates** than low anchors.

**Conclusion:** Anchoring bias operates even when the anchor is obviously implausible. Participants knew Gandhi could not have died at age 9 or 140, yet their estimates were still pulled in the direction of the anchor. This confirms that **System 1 thinking** distorts judgement before conscious reasoning can fully correct it.

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Random allocation** to conditions reduces the effect of individual differences | **Opportunity sample** from a university canteen; not representative of the wider population (likely WEIRD) |
| **Multiple questions** used across different domains increases the generalizability of the anchoring effect | **Independent samples design**: participant variability in general knowledge may have influenced estimates |
| Tests an important boundary condition: does anchoring work even with **impossible anchors**? | The study only reports summary statistics; individual differences in susceptibility to anchoring are not analyzed |
| Findings converge with **Tversky & Kahneman (1974)** and **Englich & Mussweiler (2001)**, building a consistent body of evidence | The asymmetry between low and low anchor effects is not fully explained by the study |
| Ecological relevance: implausible anchoring occurs in advertising, negotiation, and pricing contexts | **Lab setting**: answering computer-based questions does not fully reflect real-world decision-making under pressure |
