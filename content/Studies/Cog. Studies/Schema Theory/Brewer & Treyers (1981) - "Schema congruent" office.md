#study #cognitive [[Heuristics - Schema Theory]], [[Reconstructive Memory]]
___

**Brewer and Treyens (1981)** conducted a <u>Lab Experiment</u> investigating the role of schemas in the encoding and retrieval of episodic memory, demonstrating that memory for a real environment is systematically distorted by expectations derived from schemas.
___

**Aim:** To investigate the role of schema (congruence) in the encoding and retrieval of episodic memory. 

**Participants:** **86 psychology students**.

**Method:** <u>Lab experiment</u>.
- Participants were individually seated in an "office-like" room and left to wait for 35 seconds (controlled).
- All participants had the same vantage point
- The room contained a mix of:
  - **Schema-congruent objects** (expected in an office): desk, chair, shelves. 
	  Some schema-congruent objects were omitted e.g., books. 
  - **Schema-incongruent objects** (unexpected in an office): a skull, a toy top, a piece of bark
- Participants were **not told they would need to remember the room**.
- After being taken to a different room, participants were asked "Did you think that you would be asked to remember the objects in the room?" to which 93% said no
- Then they were asked to **recall the contents** of the office in detail using one of three methods:
	  - Written recall (30 participants)
	  - Drawn recall (29 participants)
	  - Verbal recall only (27 participants)
- They were also given a **recognition booklet** with 131 objects (only 61 actually in the room) and asked to rate their confidence (1-6) for each.

**Results:**
- When asked to write or draw, participants were more likely to **recall schema-congruent objects** (e.g., books, desk) present in the room. 
- **Schema-incongruent objects** (e.g., skull, bark) were not often recalled in the written or drawn recall conditions. Although participants tended to change the nature of the object to fit their schemas. 
- The recognition booklet led to higher recognition of schema-incongruent items present in the room and a higher false recognition of schema-congruent objects not present in the room.


**Conclusion:** Memory is strongly influenced by **schema**. People use their knowledge of "what an office contains" to fill in gaps in memory, sometimes accurately but often with false details. This confirms Bartlett's theory that memory is **reconstructive**, with schemas acting as scaffolding that shapes encoding and retrieval.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Deception was necessary and justified**: avoided demand characteristics; participants behaved naturally | **Deception** raises ethical concerns; participants did not consent to a memory test |
| **Pilot study** was used to pre-determine which objects were schema-consistent, increasing reliability | **Sample**: psychology undergraduates may be more aware of memory research biases |
| Directly tests **schema theory** in a realistic, ecologically valid setting (a real room) | Does not fully explain why some incongruent objects were recalled better than others |
| Consistent with **Bartlett (1932)**: schemas distort memory in predictable ways | The study used **multiple recall methods**, making direct comparison across participants difficult |
| Findings have direct applications in **eyewitness testimony** and **educational psychology** | Cannot determine whether distortions occurred at **encoding, storage, or retrieval** |

> **Key Concept - Schema Congruence & False Memory:**
> *Schemas do not just help us remember; they also cause us to misremember. Brewer & Treyens (1981) shows that we are more likely to "remember" objects that should be in a room (schema-congruent) even when they were not there. This is because memory retrieval is an active reconstruction process, using schemas to fill in what we expect, not just what we actually saw.*
