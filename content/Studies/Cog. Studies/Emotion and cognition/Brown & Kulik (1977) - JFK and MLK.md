**Aim:** To investigate whether people have particularly vivid and detailed memories for surprising and personally significant events (FBM formation).

**Participants:** **80 American adults** (40 Black, 40 White), recruited via convenience sampling. 

**Method:** <u>Standardized Questionnaire</u> (7 open questions)
- Participants were asked whether they could recall the circumstances in which they heard about the death of public figures (e.g., Jhon F. Kennedy and Martin Luther King), as well as of someone they personally knew.
- For each remembered event they described the **FBM categories**: place, activity, informant, others present, own affect, aftermath. (Details + personal involvement)
- They also rated the **personal significance** of each event.
- Racial differences were analyzed (e.g., whether Black participants had stronger FBM for MLK's assassination).

**Results:**
- **90%** of participants recalled significant amount of detail about these events. 
- **75% of Black participants** showed FBMs for **MLK's assassination**, compared to 33% of white participants.
- Events rated as more personally significant produced more vivid and complete FBMs.
	 Most participants had very detailed memories of the death of a loved one. However, there was a difference in their memories of the assassination of public officials, based on the personal relevance of the event to the participant.
- Brown & Kulik proposed FBMs are triggered by a **"Now Print!" neurological mechansm** activated by surprise and consequentiality.

**Conclusion:** Flashbulb memories are formed for events of high emotional and personal significance. The racial difference in MLK recall supports the role of **personal relevance** in determining FBM formation.

## Evaluation

| Strengths                                                               | Limitations                                                                        |
| ----------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **First systematic study** of FBM, foundational in cognitive psychology | **Self-report**: no objective way to verify accuracy of recalled memories          |
| Cross-racial comparison adds a **cultural/sociocultural dimension**     | **Retrospective**: memories were recalled after the fact, not measured at the time |
| Supported by later studies (Neisser & Harsch; Sharot et al.)            | Assumed personal significance and level of emotion.                                |
| Highlights the **role of emotion** in memory processes                  | **Sampling Bias**: limited to American adults; low generalizability                |
| Standardized tools allow replication and therefore ensure reliability   | No measure of **accuracy**: vividness does not equal correctness                   |
|                                                                         | The "Now Print!" mechanism is **theoretically speculative**                        |
|                                                                         | Questionnaire/Interview; cannot establish cause and effect relationship            |
|                                                                         | Not possible to measure role of rehearsal in the creation of memories              |
|                                                                         | Demand characteristics - **Social desirability effect**                            |

> **Key Concept - Flashbulb Memory:**
> *A flashbulb memory (FBM) is an unusually vivid and long-lasting memory for the circumstances surrounding a shocking event. Brown & Kulik coined the term and proposed the "Now Print!" mechanism. However, Neisser & Harsch (1992) challenge whether FBMs are actually accurate; they may feel vivid while still being wrong.*
