#study  #cognitive [[Reconstructive Memory]], Eyewitness testimony
___

**Yuille and Cutshall** conducted a <u>Case Study with an experimental component</u> investigating the accuracy of **eyewitness testimony** in a real-life armed robbery, directly challenging Loftus & Palmer's (1974) laboratory findings that eyewitness memory is easily distorted.

> [!Note]
> This study is best described as a **case study**, as identified by the researchers themselves in their publication. It uses method and data triangulation (experimental misleading questions + comparison to police interview transcripts). On the exam, you may describe it as an experiment (focusing on the misleading questions component) or as a case study (focusing on the full interview and archival comparison). It is **not** a natural experiment, because the researchers manipulated the IV (the misleading questions) themselves.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether leading questions would affect the memory of eyewitnesses to a real crime, and to test whether real-life eyewitness memory is more accurate and resistant to distortion than laboratory-based research (Loftus & Palmer, 1974) suggests.

**Participants:** **13 witnesses** (purposive sample) to a real gun shop robbery in Vancouver, Canada. Of the original **21 witnesses** interviewed by police, 13 agreed to take part in the study four months later.

**Background - The incident:**
A thief entered a gun shop, tied up the owner, and stole money and guns. The owner freed himself and went outside believing the thief had escaped, but the thief was still there and shot him twice. Police were called, there was an exchange of gunfire, and the thief was eventually killed. The incident occurred in front of the shop, producing 21 eyewitnesses. The researchers selected this incident because there were enough witnesses and because forensic evidence and police reports were available to verify accuracy.

**Method:** <u>Case study with experimental component</u>.
- **Four months** after the incident, Yuille & Cutshall conducted in-depth interviews with the 13 witnesses.
- Witnesses first gave a free account of the incident, then were asked questions.
- **Two misleading questions** were embedded:
  - Half were asked if they saw **"a"** broken headlight on the getaway car; the other half were asked if they saw **"the"** broken headlight. There was **no broken headlight**.
  - Half were asked if they saw **"the"** yellow quarter panel on the car; the other half were asked about **"a"** yellow quarter panel. The panel was actually **blue**.
- Participants were randomly allocated to question conditions.
- Responses were compared to the **original police interview transcripts** for accuracy.
- Witnesses also rated their **level of stress** during the incident on a **7-point scale**.

**Results:**
- Eyewitness accounts were **highly accurate**: the accuracy of witnesses' accounts compared to original police reports was between **79% and 84%**.
- **10 out of 13** witnesses correctly identified that there was no broken headlight or yellow quarter panel, or stated they had not noticed those details. This means **3 out of 13 (23%)** were misled by the leading questions.
- Witnesses who reported **higher stress** during the event showed **greater accuracy**, contradicting the assumption that stress impairs memory.
- Witnesses reported not feeling stressed during the incident but did report experiencing an **"adrenaline rush"**.

**Conclusion:** Real-life eyewitness memories for emotionally significant events can be largely accurate and resistant to distortion. However, the conclusion that this study straightforwardly contradicts Loftus & Palmer requires caution: 13% of Yuille & Cutshall's participants were misled, which is actually higher than the 19.33% misled in Loftus & Palmer's broken glass condition (29/150). The study's high accuracy figures may reflect flashbulb memory rather than general eyewitness reliability.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **High ecological validity**: real crime, real witnesses, genuine emotional stakes unlike lab video stimuli | **Purposive sample**: only 13 of 21 original witnesses agreed to participate; those who declined may have had less accurate or more distressing memories, biasing results |
| **Archival evidence** (original police transcripts) provides an objective baseline for measuring accuracy | Witnesses may have **rehearsed** memories through media coverage, social discussion, or personal reflection over the four months before interview |
| Uses **method triangulation**: experimental misleading questions combined with interview comparison to police records | **23% of participants were misled** (3/13), which is actually higher than the 19.33% misled in Loftus & Palmer — this complicates the conclusion that the study contradicts Loftus & Palmer |
| Directly tests whether **leading questions** distort real-world memory, replicating Loftus & Palmer's aims in a naturalistic context | Cannot control for **individual differences** in vantage point, proximity to the incident, or prior experience with crime |
| **Stress enhancement finding**: higher stress was associated with greater accuracy, challenging the assumption that arousal impairs memory | The study may reflect **flashbulb memory** rather than general eyewitness reliability, limiting generalizability to less emotionally significant events |

> **Key Concept - Does Yuille & Cutshall Really Contradict Loftus & Palmer?**
> *The study is often cited as a straightforward counter-study to Loftus & Palmer. The PDF source notes an important complication: 3 out of 13 witnesses (23%) were misled by the leading questions, which is actually a higher proportion than the 19.33% misled in Loftus & Palmer's broken glass condition. The high overall accuracy (79-84%) may reflect flashbulb memory encoding during the emotionally intense incident, rather than general resistance to leading questions. This makes Yuille & Cutshall a nuanced study: it supports ecological validity as a research concern, but does not cleanly prove that real-life memory is immune to distortion.*
