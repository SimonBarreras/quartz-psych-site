**Elizabeth Loftus and Jacqueline Pickrell** conducted a <u>Mixed-Methods Study</u> investigating whether **false autobiographical memories** can be implanted through suggestion, a classic demonstration of the malleability of long-term memory.

> **Useful for:** *"Discuss ethical considerations in cognitive research"* / *"To what extent are cognitive processes reliable?"* / *"Discuss reconstructive memory"*

___

**Aim:** To investigate whether false memories of childhood events can be created in adults through written suggestion and repeated interviewing.

**Participants:** **24 participants** (2 male, 22 female) recruited with the help of a relative who provided information about real childhood events.

**Method:** <u>Mixed-methods study</u> combining questionnaire and semi-structured interviews.

**Procedure:**
1. A **relative** of each participant was contacted and asked to provide 3 genuine childhood memories and confirm that the participant had **never been lost in a shopping mall** as a child.
2. Participants received a **booklet** containing 4 short memory descriptions: **3 real** (provided by the relative) and **1 false** (getting lost in a shopping mall at age 5, reunited with a family member by an elderly woman).
3. Participants were asked to **write what they remembered** about each event or write "I don't remember this."
4. **Two follow-up interviews** were conducted approximately **1-2 weeks apart**, asking participants to recall and elaborate on the four events.
5. After the second interview, participants were **debriefed** and asked to identify which memory they thought was false.

**Results:**
- **25% of participants (6 out of 24)** claimed to remember the false mall event, either fully or partially.
- Those who "remembered" the false event rated it with **lower confidence** than real memories, but still described it in detail.
- During debriefing, most participants were **surprised** to learn the mall memory was fabricated; some initially resisted the debriefing.

**Conclusion:** False autobiographical memories **can be created through suggestion**, even for events that never occurred. This demonstrates that long-term memory is **reconstructive and vulnerable to external influence**, with serious implications for clinical settings (e.g., recovered memory therapy) and legal contexts.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Data triangulation**: family-verified real memories provide an objective baseline | **Small, unrepresentative sample** (24, mostly female); limits generalizability |
| **High ecological validity**: real autobiographical memories, filled in at home | Participants filled questionnaires at **home**, introducing possible contamination and bias |
| Findings have been **replicated and extended** (e.g., Shaw, 2015; false memories of crime) | Difficult to distinguish a **true false memory** from imaginative compliance ("going along") |
| **Real-world relevance**: implications for therapy, police interviews, and legal testimony | **Ethical concerns**: participants were deceived; learning their memories were false could be distressing |
| Triangulation of methods (questionnaire + two interviews) strengthens findings | The false event (mall) was relatively **plausible**; may not generalize to more bizarre implanted memories |

> **Key Concept - False Memory & Reconstructive Memory:**
> *Loftus & Pickrell (1995) shows that memory is not simply retrieved but actively reconstructed, and that reconstruction is vulnerable to suggestion. This has critical implications: if false childhood memories can be implanted through a simple written description, the reliability of recovered memories in therapy and eyewitness testimony in court must be questioned carefully.*
