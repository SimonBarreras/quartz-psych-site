#Study #Biological [[Neurotransmission]]
___

**Antonova et al.** conducted a <u>Double-Blind Repeated Measures Experiment</u> using **fMRI** to investigate the role of **acetylcholine (ACh)** in **spatial memory** and **hippocampal activation** in humans, extending animal research findings to a human sample.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether blocking ACh receptors using scopolamine reduces hippocampal activation during a spatial memory task in healthy human participants.

**Participants:** **20 healthy male adults**, mean age **28 years**.

**Method:** <u>Double-blind, placebo-controlled, repeated measures experiment</u>.
- Participants were injected with either **scopolamine** (a muscarinic ACh antagonist) or a **saline placebo** **70-90 minutes** before the task.
- They then performed the **"Arena task"**: a virtual reality spatial navigation game in which participants navigate an arena to reach a pole, remember its location over a 30-second blank screen, and then navigate to it again from a new starting point.
- Participants were first trained on the task to ensure familiarity with the controls.
- Brain activity was measured via **fMRI** across **six trials**.
- Participants returned **3-4 weeks later** and completed the task again under the **opposite condition** (counterbalanced repeated measures design).

**Results:**
- Participants in the scopolamine condition showed **significantly reduced hippocampal activation** compared to the placebo condition.
- The error rate in the scopolamine condition was higher, but this difference was **not statistically significant**.
- The fMRI data provided the critical finding: without neuroimaging, the biological effect would have been undetectable from behaviour alone.

**Conclusion:** ACh plays a significant role in hippocampal activation during spatial memory encoding in humans. The study extends animal findings (Rogers & Kesner) to humans and demonstrates the value of combining pharmacological manipulation with neuroimaging.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Repeated measures design** eliminates participant variability | **Small sample** (20 males only): low statistical power and cannot generalise to women |
| **Counterbalancing** (opposite condition 3-4 weeks later) controls for practice effects | **Several participants reported stress** from the injection or fMRI enclosure, which may itself have impaired hippocampal functioning |
| **Double-blind design** eliminates experimenter and participant bias | The **behavioural performance difference was not significant**: only the fMRI measure showed a significant difference, suggesting the Arena task may not have been sensitive enough |
| **fMRI provides objective biological data** beyond what behaviour alone can reveal | **Scopolamine** affects multiple neurotransmitter systems, not ACh alone; difficult to fully isolate ACh's specific contribution |
| **Human participants** overcome the generalisability limitation of animal studies | **Ethical concerns**: administering an unfamiliar drug to healthy participants carries medical risk |

> **Key Concept - Why fMRI Mattered Here:**
> *The behavioural performance difference between scopolamine and placebo was not statistically significant. Only the fMRI revealed a significant reduction in hippocampal activation. This is an important methodological point: without brain imaging, the role of ACh would have appeared negligible from behaviour alone. It demonstrates why combining pharmacological manipulation with neuroimaging is more powerful than either method in isolation.*
