 [[Localization of function]], #Biological  #Study
___

* **Milner** conducted a <u>Longitudinal Case Study</u> on **Henry Molaison (HM)**, one of the most studied individuals in the history of neuroscience, whose specific pattern of memory loss following brain surgery provided landmark evidence for the **localization of memory function** and the role of the hippocampus.
___

**Background:**
- Henry Molaison suffered a cycling accident at age **7**, leading to severe epilepsy from age **10**.
- By age **27**, seizures had become so debilitating that they prevented a normal life.
- With **family consent**, HM underwent experimental surgery in which sections of the **medial temporal lobe** (including most of the **hippocampus**, amygdala, and entorhinal cortex) were bilaterally removed.
- The surgery successfully reduced seizures, but produced unexpected and profound memory changes.

**Aim:** To investigate and document the nature of HM's memory impairment following bilateral hippocampal removal, and what this reveals about the role of the hippocampus in memory.

**Method:** <u>Case study</u> using method triangulation:
- Psychometric testing (IQ testing; HM's IQ remained in the normal range)
- Direct behavioural observation
- Structured interviews
- Cognitive memory testing (recall, recognition, learning tasks)
- MRI scanning by Corkin (1997) to confirm extent of damage

**Results - HM's Memory Profile:**

| Memory Type | Intact? | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Long-term memories (pre-surgery)** | Intact | Childhood and pre-surgery memories were largely preserved |
| **Procedural memory** | Intact | Could learn new motor skills (e.g., mirror drawing task), improving with practice even without remembering doing it |
| **Short-term memory (working memory)** | Intact | Could hold information briefly in mind |
| **New episodic memory** | Impaired | Could not form new memories of events (anterograde amnesia) |
| **New semantic memory** | Impaired | Could not learn new factual information |

**Key Insight:** HM could improve on motor tasks through practice, showing **procedural memory is preserved**, but would have no recollection of having practised, demonstrating a clear dissociation between explicit (declarative) and implicit (procedural) memory systems.

**Conclusion:** The **hippocampus is not the site of permanent memory storage** but is essential for **consolidating and organizing new explicit memories** (episodic and semantic). Without it, new declarative memories cannot be formed, but procedural learning remains intact.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Method triangulation** (psychometric, observational, interview, MRI) increases validity and reliability | **Single case study**: HM's brain damage was unique; cannot generalize directly |
| Provided the first clear evidence for **separate memory systems** (explicit vs. implicit) | HM's surgery removed multiple structures, making it impossible to isolate the hippocampus alone |
| **Longitudinal**: HM was studied for over 40 years, providing rich, detailed data | **Ethical concerns**: HM could not consent to ongoing research due to his amnesia (family consented) |
| Revolutionized the neuroscience of memory and inspired decades of research | HM may have had pre-existing abnormalities due to long-term epilepsy |
| Consistent with **Maguire (2000)** and **Draganski (2004)** on hippocampal function | Cannot establish exactly what each removed region contributed individually |

> **Key Concept - Anterograde Amnesia:**
> *Anterograde amnesia is the inability to form new long-term memories after a brain injury, while old memories remain intact. HM's case proved that the hippocampus is essential for the consolidation of new explicit (declarative) memories. It also demonstrated that procedural memory (implicit) is stored elsewhere, likely in the basal ganglia and cerebellum, leading to the modern understanding of multiple memory systems.*
