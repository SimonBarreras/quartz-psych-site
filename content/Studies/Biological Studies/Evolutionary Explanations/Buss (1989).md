#Study #Biological [[Evolutionary Explanations - Human Relationships]], [[Hormones In Human Behaviour]]
___

**David Buss** conducted a <u>Cross-Cultural Survey</u> with the aim of investigating whether **sex differences in mate preferences** are universal across cultures, supporting an **evolutionary (biological) explanation** for human relationship formation.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether men and women differ in their mate preferences in ways predicted by evolutionary theory (parental investment and sexual selection), and whether these differences are universal across cultures.

**Participants:** Over **10,000 participants** from **37 cultures** across 6 continents and 5 islands, making it one of the largest cross-cultural studies in psychology.

**Method:** <u>Cross-cultural self-report survey</u>.
- Participants rated and ranked **18 characteristics** desired in a long-term mate (e.g., financial prospects, physical attractiveness, chastity, ambition, age).
- They also indicated their preferred age difference between themselves and a partner.
- Questionnaires were translated and back-translated to ensure accuracy.

**Results:**
- **Women** consistently valued **financial resources, ambition, and older age** in a mate more than men, across nearly all 37 cultures.
- **Men** consistently valued **physical attractiveness and youth** more than women, also across nearly all cultures.
- Men preferred partners approximately **2.5 years younger**; women preferred partners approximately **3.5 years older**.
- Differences were statistically significant and consistent across vastly different cultures.

**Conclusion:** The universality of sex differences in mate preferences supports an **evolutionary explanation**: preferences reflect evolved reproductive strategies shaped by **parental investment** (Trivers, 1972). Women invest more biologically (pregnancy, nursing) and select mates with resources and status; men prioritize cues to fertility (youth, physical attractiveness).

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Cross-cultural scope** (37 cultures) greatly increases **generalizability** | **Self-report bias**: social desirability may distort responses |
| **Large sample size** (10,000+) increases **statistical reliability** | Survey cannot establish cause-and-effect (correlation is not causation) |
| Consistency across radically different cultures strengthens the biological argument | Concepts like "financial prospects" may not translate equally across all cultures |
| Supports **Trivers' Parental Investment Theory** | Ignores socialization; Eagly & Wood (1999) argue social roles, not biology, explain the differences |
| Standardized, replicable method | Demand characteristics: participants may have guessed the study's aim |

> **Key Concept - Parental Investment Theory (Trivers, 1972):**
> *Women invest more in offspring biologically, so they are more selective in mate choice. Men invest less biologically, so they tend to maximize reproductive opportunities. This drives the differing mate preferences observed universally across cultures.*
