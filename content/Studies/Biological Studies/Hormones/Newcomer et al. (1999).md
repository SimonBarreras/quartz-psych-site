#Study #Biological [[Hormones In Human Behaviour]]
___

**John Newcomer et al.** conducted a <u>Double-Blind Placebo-Controlled Experiment</u> investigating the effect of **cortisol** (a stress hormone) on **verbal declarative memory** in healthy human participants, directly testing the causal role of cortisol in cognitive impairment.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether elevated cortisol levels, comparable to those found during major stress or illness, impair verbal declarative memory in healthy adults.

**Participants:** **51 healthy adults** randomly assigned to three conditions. Participants had no history of psychiatric or medical conditions that could affect cortisol levels or memory.

**Method:** <u>Double-blind, placebo-controlled experiment</u> with independent groups design.

| Group | Condition | Cortisol dose |
|---|---|---|
| **Group 1** | High-dose cortisol | 160 mg/day for 4 days |
| **Group 2** | Low-dose cortisol | 40 mg/day for 4 days |
| **Group 3** | Placebo | Sugar pill for 4 days |

- After 4 days of treatment, participants completed a **verbal declarative memory test**: they listened to a prose passage and were tested on recall of its content.
- Blood samples were taken to verify cortisol levels in each condition.
- Participants and experimenters were blind to condition assignment.

**Results:**
- The **high-dose cortisol group** showed **significantly worse verbal declarative memory** than both the low-dose and placebo groups.
- The **low-dose cortisol group** showed **no significant difference** from the placebo group.
- The memory impairment in the high-dose group was **reversible**: when cortisol administration stopped, memory performance returned to baseline levels.
- Blood cortisol levels in the high-dose condition were comparable to those found in patients experiencing **major medical illness or severe psychological stress**.

**Conclusion:** High levels of cortisol, equivalent to those produced during severe stress, **causally impair verbal declarative memory** in healthy adults. The effect is dose-dependent and reversible, suggesting cortisol interferes with hippocampal memory consolidation rather than causing permanent damage.

---

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Double-blind design** eliminates experimenter and participant bias | **Artificial administration**: cortisol was taken orally, which differs from the natural stress-induced release via the HPA axis |
| **Experimental design** with random allocation allows cause-and-effect conclusions | **Short duration** (4 days): does not reflect the effects of chronic stress-related cortisol elevation |
| **Reversibility finding** rules out permanent damage; isolates the mechanism to temporary hippocampal disruption | **Healthy adult sample** may not represent those with chronic stress conditions, where cortisol effects may differ |
| Cortisol doses were **clinically validated** to match real-world stress levels, increasing ecological relevance | Only verbal declarative memory was tested; other memory types and cognitive functions were not assessed |
| Supports the broader literature on the **HPA axis and stress-memory relationship** | **Ethical concerns**: administering high doses of cortisol to healthy participants carries medical risk |

> **Key Concept - Cortisol & the Hippocampus:**
> *Cortisol is the primary stress hormone released via the HPA axis (hypothalamus, pituitary gland, adrenal cortex). While short-term cortisol release can enhance memory consolidation for emotionally significant events (cf. McGaugh & Cahill), prolonged or excessive cortisol exposure impairs the hippocampus, the region critical for declarative memory. Newcomer et al. (1999) demonstrates this causal relationship directly: high cortisol impairs verbal memory in a dose-dependent, reversible manner.*
