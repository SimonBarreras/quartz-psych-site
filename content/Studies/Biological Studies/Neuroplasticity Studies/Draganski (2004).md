#biological #Study  [[Neuroplasticity]]
___

**Draganski et al.** conducted a <u>Field Experiment using MRI scans</u> to investigate whether **learning a new motor skill** (juggling) produces measurable **structural changes in grey matter**, directly demonstrating both neuroplasticity and neural pruning in the same participants.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether learning to juggle causes structural changes in grey matter density in the brain, and whether these changes reverse after training stops, demonstrating both plasticity and synaptic pruning.

**Participants:** **24 volunteers** (21 female, 3 male), aged **20-24**, with no prior juggling experience, randomly assigned to:
- **Juggling group** (n = 12): taught a three-ball cascade juggling routine and asked to practice until mastered
- **Non-juggling control group** (n = 12): no training throughout the study

**Method:** <u>Field experiment</u> with 3 MRI scan time points. Because participants practiced at home, the IV was manipulated under natural rather than fully controlled conditions, classifying this as a field experiment.
- **Scan 1 (Baseline):** Before training began; both groups scanned to confirm no initial grey matter differences.
- **Scan 2 (Post-learning):** After the juggling group notified researchers they had mastered the routine; control group scanned again at the same time.
- **Scan 3 (After 3-month rest):** Both groups scanned again after the juggling group stopped practicing.
- **Voxel-based morphometry (VBM)** was used to measure changes in grey matter density across the brain.

**Results:**
- At baseline, there were **no significant differences** in grey matter between the two groups.
- After learning, the juggling group showed **significantly increased grey matter density** in the **mid-temporal area (V5/MT) in both hemispheres**, a region associated with visual motion processing and the perception and spatial anticipation of moving objects.
- The **control group showed no changes** throughout the study.
- After the 3-month rest period, the grey matter in the jugglers **partially decreased** back toward baseline, demonstrating **synaptic pruning**.
- Notably, changes appeared in the visual memory area rather than the cerebellum or basal ganglia, suggesting juggling relies more on **visual-spatial memory** than on procedural memory.

**Conclusion:** Learning a new skill causes measurable structural changes in grey matter in areas relevant to that skill, driven by **Long Term Potentiation (LTP)**: repeated activation of neural pathways strengthens synaptic connections and produces dendritic branching. When training stops, reduced potentiation leads to pruning. This demonstrates that neuroplasticity operates in both directions.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Experimental design** with random assignment; can establish cause-and-effect between juggling and grey matter changes | **Small sample** (24 participants); limited statistical power; results may not be reliable due to averaging across few participants |
| **Three scan time points** allow tracking of both growth and pruning over time in the same participants | **Field experiment**: participants practiced at home, introducing lack of control over confounding variables and reducing internal validity |
| **Objective VBM measures** avoid self-report bias | **Gender imbalance** (21 female, 3 male); findings may not generalise equally across sexes |
| Directly demonstrates both **neuroplasticity and pruning** in the same study | VBM methodology has known limitations in precision of grey matter measurement |
| Consistent with **Maguire (2000)** and **Rosenzweig et al. (1972)** on experience-driven structural change | Juggling is a specific visuomotor skill; findings may not generalise to other types of learning |

> **Key Concept - Long Term Potentiation (LTP) and Pruning:**
> *LTP is the process by which repeated activation of a neural pathway strengthens synaptic connections and leads to dendritic branching, increasing grey matter density. Draganski (2004) shows this happening in real time: learning juggling produced measurable grey matter growth in visual motion areas. When training stopped, reduced potentiation led to pruning, a partial reversal of those changes. This makes Draganski the most direct demonstration in the vault of both halves of the "use it or lose it" principle.*
