#PSY #Study #HRelations [[Human Relationships]]
___

**Thomas Bradbury and Frank Fincham** conducted a <u>Cross-Sectional Observational Study</u> investigating the role of **causal and responsibility attributions** in marital satisfaction, identifying how couples explain each other's behaviour and whether this pattern correlates with relationship quality.
___

**Background:** Bradbury & Fincham identified two distinct attributional communication patterns in couples:

| Pattern | Negative behaviours | Positive behaviours | Typical of |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Relationship-enhancing** | Attributed to situational factors; partner not blamed | Credited to the partner's character | Happy couples |
| **Distress-maintaining** | Attributed to partner's character; seen as intentional and selfish | Dismissed as luck or circumstance | Unhappy couples |

**Aim:** To investigate whether these attributional patterns correlate with marital satisfaction and to identify the communication behaviours that distinguish satisfied from dissatisfied couples.

---

**Participants:** **47 married couples** recruited via local media advertisements. All were cohabiting, had received no prior marriage counselling, and had a mean marriage length of **8.5 years**. All completed the **Marital Adjustment Test (MAT)**.

**Method:** <u>Cross-sectional observational study</u>.
- Each spouse **individually** identified their greatest marital problems and indicated the causes and responsibility for those problems.
- Couples were then brought together to **discuss a common problem for 15 minutes** in a laboratory setting (video recorded).
- **Three trained researchers** independently coded the videos for communication patterns (researcher triangulation).

---

**Results:**
- Couples with **lower marital satisfaction** showed significantly more **distress-maintaining communication**:
  - More likely to attribute problems to their partner's character.
  - More likely to view partner behaviour as intentional and selfish.
  - Responded with greater hostility and rejection of the partner's positive approaches.
- Couples with **higher satisfaction** showed relationship-enhancing patterns, making generous situational attributions for negative behaviour and crediting partners for positive behaviour.

**Conclusion:** Attributional style is strongly associated with marital satisfaction. Distress-maintaining patterns, where partners interpret negativity as intentional and discount positivity, are a hallmark of unhappy relationships.

---

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Researcher triangulation** (3 independent coders) increases coding reliability | **Correlational / cross-sectional**: cannot determine whether distress-maintaining communication causes dissatisfaction or results from it |
| Uses both **self-report and behavioural observation** (methodological triangulation) | **Questionnaire administered before observation**: may have influenced behaviour (order effects; not counterbalanced) |
| Identifies a **specific, actionable cognitive mechanism**; useful for therapeutic application | Problems discussed varied in severity; distressed couples discussed more difficult issues (confounding variable) |
| **Researcher triangulation** adds objectivity to subjective video coding | Other variables not measured (e.g., depression) may have influenced communication style |
| Findings align with broader **attribution theory** in cognitive psychology | **Western sample** only; limits cross-cultural generalisability |

> **Key Concept - Attribution in Relationships:**
> *Attribution refers to how we explain the causes of behaviour. Bradbury & Fincham (1992) showed that happy couples and unhappy couples interpret the same behaviours in systematically different ways. Happy couples make charitable attributions: situational for bad behaviour, personal for good. Unhappy couples do the reverse. This cognitive pattern is both a symptom of dissatisfaction and a mechanism that deepens it over time.*
