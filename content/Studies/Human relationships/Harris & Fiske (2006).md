#Study #HRelations [[Human Relationships]], [[Stereotypes]]
___

**Susan Fiske and Lasana Harris** conducted a <u>Lab Experiment using fMRI</u> investigating whether certain social outgroups are **dehumanized at a neural level**, specifically whether they fail to activate the brain regions normally involved in social cognition, providing neurobiological evidence for the role of prejudice in perception.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether images of extreme social outgroups (homeless people and drug addicts) fail to activate the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), a region associated with social cognition and mentalizing, compared to images of other social groups.

**Participants:** **22 Princeton undergraduates**. The group was divided into two halves: one half viewed images of people, the other viewed images of objects.

**Method:** <u>Lab experiment</u> using fMRI neuroimaging.
- Before scanning, participants practised rating neutral photos for four emotions: **pride, envy, pity, and disgust**, as a control to ensure they understood the task.
- Inside the fMRI, participants viewed **six sets of ten photographs** depicting individuals from various social groups, including:
  - People with disabilities
  - Rich businessmen
  - Older people
  - American Olympic athletes
  - Homeless people / drug addicts
- After each image, participants used a **joystick** to indicate which of the four emotions they felt toward the person shown.
- Brain activity was measured, focusing specifically on the **medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC)**, the **amygdala**, and the **insula**.

> Note: The SCM group assignments in the original note (e.g., "elderly = high-warmth, high-competence") were not specified in the published procedure and should not be presented as findings of this study.

**Results:**
- Images of **all social groups** activated the mPFC and standard social cognition networks, **except** images of homeless people and drug addicts.
- The **low-warmth, low-competence group** (homeless/drug addicts) produced **significantly reduced mPFC activation**, closer to the pattern seen when viewing **objects** than when viewing other humans.
- These images instead activated the **insula** and **amygdala**, regions associated with disgust and threat responses.
- The behavioural data supported this: participants responded **more slowly and less accurately** when judging the preferences of homeless/drug addicts, suggesting social cognition was not automatically engaged.

**Conclusion:** Extreme social outgroups associated with disgust are **dehumanized at a neural level**: they do not automatically trigger the brain circuitry normally reserved for thinking about other people. This has implications for understanding how prejudice enables harm and neglect of certain social groups.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Objective fMRI measures** provide biological evidence for dehumanization beyond self-report | **Small sample** (22 undergraduates from Princeton); fMRI cost constrains sample size and limits generalizability, especially to populations with different cultural backgrounds |
| **Within-subjects design** controls for individual differences in baseline brain activity | **Artificial task**: viewing photographs in an fMRI scanner does not replicate real-world social encounters |
| Identifies a **specific neural mechanism** (mPFC suppression) underlying prejudice | Cannot confirm that reduced mPFC activity represents dehumanization; alternative explanations (e.g., unfamiliarity, low expected interaction) exist |
| Links social psychological concepts (warmth-competence model) to **neuroscience** | **WEIRD sample**: Princeton undergraduates; attitudes toward homeless people and drug addicts may differ across cultures and socioeconomic groups |
| Has significant **applied implications** for understanding how neglect and harm toward vulnerable groups is cognitively enabled | The study identifies a correlate of prejudice, not its cause; cannot establish whether the neural response drives discriminatory behaviour |

> **Key Concept - Neural Dehumanization:**
> *Harris & Fiske (2006) demonstrates that dehumanization is not merely a social or rhetorical phenomenon but has a measurable neural basis. When we encounter individuals associated with extreme disgust, the brain processes them less as people and more as objects, bypassing the circuitry we use for social thinking. This neural shortcut may explain how prejudice enables neglect and harm: if a person does not automatically trigger our social cognition system, empathy and moral concern are less likely to be activated.*
