#PSY #HRelations [[Human Relationships]]
___

**Knapp and Vangelisti's (1996) Model of Relational Development** is a stage model describing how relationships escalate and terminate. It proposes that relationships move through predictable stages in both directions: toward greater intimacy and away from it. The model is relevant to IB Psychology for questions on the **formation, maintenance, change, and dissolution of personal relationships**.

---

## Relationship Escalation (Coming Together)

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| **1. Initiating** | The very first contact. Focused on making a favourable impression. Physical appearance plays a significant role. Judgements at this stage tend to be inaccurate. |
| **2. Experimenting** | Getting to know each other through small talk and searching for common ground. Partners decide whether to continue the relationship. Most relationships end here. |
| **3. Intensifying** | The relationship becomes less formal and more personal. Self-disclosure increases: personal information, feelings, and revelations are shared. Partners invest in nurturing the relationship through gifts, time, and asking for commitment. |
| **4. Integrating** | Partners begin thinking and planning together. Use of "we" becomes more frequent. Those outside the relationship start recognising them as a couple. |
| **5. Bonding** | Public announcement of the relationship through formal rituals such as engagement, marriage, or friendship ceremonies. Commitment is made. |

---

## Relationship Termination (Coming Apart)

| Stage | Description |
|---|---|
| **1. Differentiating** | Partners begin thinking individually rather than as a couple. Differences become more visible and independence is desired. Feelings of dislike may begin to be expressed; partners may resent their commitment. |
| **2. Circumscribing** | Communication becomes more restricted. Partners avoid difficult topics and set up personal boundaries. Conversations are more limited in scope and depth. |
| **3. Stagnating** | Communication is minimal. Partners may stay together due to external constraints (children, finances, friends) or to avoid the pain of separation, even when the relationship has effectively ended emotionally. |
| **4. Avoiding** | Partners intentionally minimise contact and become physically and emotionally detached. Communication is restricted to avoid confrontation or conversation entirely. |
| **5. Terminating** | Partners decide to separate. The termination is not always a deliberate decision like divorce; it can occur naturally through circumstances (moving away, end of shared living situation). |

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Provides a **clear, structured framework** for understanding how relationships develop and decline over time | The model is **linear and sequential**: real relationships do not always move neatly through stages in order; they may skip stages or oscillate between them |
| **Applies to multiple relationship types**: romantic, friendships, and professional relationships | Developed primarily in a **Western, individualist cultural context**; may not reflect relationship development in collectivist cultures where social and family approval plays a greater role |
| Useful for understanding research findings: Gottman's Four Horsemen map onto the Differentiating and Circumscribing stages; Felmlee's fatal attractions often trigger the Differentiating stage | The model is **descriptive, not explanatory**: it identifies what happens but does not explain why relationships move through these stages |
| Self-disclosure in the Intensifying stage is consistent with **social penetration theory** (Altman & Taylor) | **Difficult to test empirically**: stage boundaries are fuzzy and self-report of relationship stage is subjective |

---

> **Key Concept - Self-Disclosure and Relationship Depth:**
> *The Intensifying stage is where self-disclosure becomes central. As partners reveal more personal information, they become more vulnerable and the relationship deepens. This is consistent with Social Penetration Theory's "onion model": relationships develop as partners peel back successive layers of self-disclosure. Bradbury & Fincham (1992) shows that the quality of communication at this stage predicts long-term satisfaction, while Gottman (1999) demonstrates that patterns established early in a relationship (the Integrating and Bonding stages) predict whether the Differentiating stage will eventually follow.*
