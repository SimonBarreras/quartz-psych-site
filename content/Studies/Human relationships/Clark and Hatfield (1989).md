#HRelations #Study #Biological #Sociocultural [[Evolutionary Explanations - Human Relationships]], [[Human Relationships]]
___

**Russell Clark and Elaine Hatfield** conducted a <u>Field Experiment</u> investigating **gender differences in receptivity to casual sexual offers** from strangers, testing evolutionary predictions about sex differences in short-term mating strategies.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether men and women differ in their willingness to accept offers of a date, an apartment visit, or casual sex from an attractive stranger, and whether this supports evolutionary explanations for sex differences in mating behaviour.

**Participants:** Male and female college students at **Florida State University**, approached on campus by attractive confederates of the opposite sex.

**Method:** <u>Field experiment</u>.
- Attractive male and female **confederates** approached opposite-sex strangers on campus with the opening line: *"I've been noticing you around campus. I find you very attractive."*
- They then made one of three requests:
  1. *"Would you go out with me tonight?"*
  2. *"Would you come over to my apartment tonight?"*
  3. *"Would you go to bed with me tonight?"*
- Each confederate approached **48 opposite-sex strangers** per question condition.
- Acceptance rates were recorded for each gender across all three conditions.

**Results:**

| Request | % Men saying yes | % Women saying yes |
|---|---|---|
| Go out with me? | 50% | 56% |
| Come to my apartment? | 69% | 6% |
| Go to bed with me? | **75%** | **0%** |

- Men were significantly more willing to accept a sexual proposition than women.
- Women were slightly more willing to go on a date, but dramatically less willing to agree to sex.
- Men who declined the sexual offer often gave reasons like *"I have a girlfriend"*, suggesting willingness in principle.
- Women who declined often expressed discomfort or concern for their safety.

**Conclusion:** There is a **substantial gender difference in short-term mating receptivity**, consistent with **evolutionary theory**: men maximize reproductive success by accepting more sexual opportunities; women are more selective due to greater biological investment (pregnancy, nursing). This supports **Parental Investment Theory** (Trivers, 1972).

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **High ecological validity**: real campus setting, real behaviour | **Lack of informed consent**: participants did not know they were in a study |
| Simple, clear experimental design with **strong, unambiguous results** | **Cultural bias**: conducted in 1980s USA; may not generalize to other cultures or eras |
| Directly tests **evolutionary predictions** in a real-world context | **Heteronormative**: only studied male-female interactions; excludes LGBTQ+ perspectives |
| Results have been **replicated** in other cultures with similar findings | **Social desirability**: women may have felt social pressure to decline; men to accept |
| Supports and extends **Buss (1989)** cross-cultural findings | Safety concerns may explain women's refusals, not purely mating strategy |

> **Key Concept - Parental Investment & Short-Term Mating:**
> *Parental Investment Theory (Trivers, 1972) predicts that the sex investing more in offspring (females) will be more selective in mate choice, while the less-investing sex (males) will seek more partners. Clark & Hatfield (1989) provides striking real-world evidence for this: 75% of men vs. 0% of women agreed to casual sex with an attractive stranger.*
