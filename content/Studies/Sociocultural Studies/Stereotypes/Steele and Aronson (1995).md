#Study #Sociocultural [[Stereotypes]], [[Social Identity Theory]]
___

**Steele and Aronson** conducted a <u>Lab Experiment</u> investigating **stereotype threat**, the phenomenon where awareness of a negative stereotype about one's social group impairs performance on tasks relevant to that stereotype.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether making Black students aware of a racial stereotype about intellectual ability would negatively affect their performance on a standardized verbal test, compared to when the test was not framed in a stereotype-threatening way.

**Participants:** **114 male and female Black and White undergraduates** from Stanford University. SAT verbal scores were collected prior to the study to ensure all participants were within the norm of verbal performance, minimizing participant variability.

**Method:** <u>Lab experiment</u> with independent groups design.
- All participants completed a **standardized test of verbal ability, similar to the SAT**.
- There were **two independent variables**:
  1. **Race of the participant** (Black or White)
  2. **Test framing** (diagnostic vs. non-diagnostic)
- Participants were randomly assigned to one of two conditions with equal numbers in each:
  - **Stereotype threat condition (diagnostic)**: Told the test was a measure of **intellectual ability**, activating the stereotype that Black students perform worse academically.
  - **Non-threat condition (non-diagnostic)**: Told the test was a **problem-solving task**, with no implications for intellectual ability.
- Performance scores were compared across conditions and racial groups.
- There was **no significant difference between male and female participants**, so gender was not included in the analysis.

**Results:**
- In the **stereotype threat condition**, **Black students performed significantly worse** than White students of equivalent SAT scores.
- In the **non-threat condition**, the performance gap between Black and White students **virtually disappeared**; Black students performed equally well to White students.
- White students' performance was **not significantly affected** by the framing.

**Conclusion:** Mere awareness of a negative stereotype about one's group is sufficient to impair cognitive performance, a phenomenon Steele & Aronson termed **stereotype threat**. The threat is proposed to consume cognitive resources (attention, working memory) needed for the task, reducing performance. However, this mechanism was not directly measured in this first variation of the experiment.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Controlled lab experiment**: random assignment to conditions establishes cause-and-effect between framing and performance | **Stanford student sample**: not representative of all Black Americans; limits generalizability |
| **SAT score screening** minimizes participant variability in prior verbal ability | **Independent samples design**: a matched-pairs design may have been a better approach to control for individual differences |
| Has been **widely replicated** across many groups (women in maths, elderly in memory tasks) | **Stereotype threat was not directly measured** in this first variation: racial identity salience and stress levels during the exam were not assessed; the performance difference alone does not confirm stereotype threat was the mechanism |
| Highly **impactful** in education, policy, and social psychology | **Demand characteristics**: participants may have inferred the study's purpose from the framing |
| Highlights how **social identity and stereotypes** affect cognitive performance | Effect size varies across replications; some meta-analyses find smaller effects than the original study |

> **Key Concept - Stereotype Threat:**
> *Stereotype threat occurs when an individual is in a situation where they risk confirming a negative stereotype about their social group. This awareness creates psychological pressure that is proposed to divert cognitive resources away from the task, reducing performance. Critically, the threat does not require the individual to personally believe the stereotype; awareness of its existence is sufficient. However, Steele & Aronson (1995) did not directly measure stereotype threat in this first variation; later variations of the experiment confirmed the mechanism.*
