**Joy, Kimball and Zabrack** conducted a <u>Natural Experiment</u> investigating the effect of **television introduction** on children's aggressive behaviour in a real Canadian community, providing naturalistic evidence for **Social Cognitive Learning Theory (SCLT)** in a non-laboratory setting.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether the introduction of television to a community that previously had no TV access would lead to increases in children's aggressive behaviour, compared to communities that already had TV.

**Participants:** Children from **three Canadian towns**, studied longitudinally:
- **Notel** ("no television"): A town that had never received TV reception due to geography, about to receive TV for the first time.
- **Unitel**: A town with access to one TV channel.
- **Multitel**: A town with access to multiple TV channels.

**Method:** <u>Natural experiment</u> with longitudinal observational and rating measures.
- **Phase 1 (baseline)**: Children's aggressive behaviour was measured **before** Notel received TV, and at the same time in Unitel and Multitel as comparison points.
- **Phase 2 (follow-up, 2 years later)**: The same measures were taken **after** Notel had received TV for 2 years.
- **Measures of aggression:**
  - Direct observation of children in the playground (physical and verbal aggression coded)
  - Peer ratings of aggression
  - Teacher ratings of aggression

**Results:**
- **Notel**: Children's observed physical aggression increased by approximately **160%** after 2 years of TV access, a dramatic and statistically significant rise.
- **Unitel and Multitel**: No significant increase in aggression over the same period.
- Increases in aggression were found for **both boys and girls**, and for **both physical and verbal aggression**.
- There was **no significant difference** in favourite shows between more and less aggressive children, suggesting it was general TV exposure, not specific violent content, that mattered.
- Males were more physically aggressive than females overall.

**Interpretation:**
- The most likely explanation was **observational learning** (SCLT): children in Notel learned new aggressive scripts and behaviours from TV models.
- An alternative interpretation was **arousal**: novelty of TV may have heightened general arousal, leading to increased aggression. However, this alone does not explain the sustained 2-year increase.

**Conclusion:** The introduction of television caused a significant increase in children's aggressive behaviour, providing strong naturalistic support for **SCLT**, that aggression is learned through observation of media models.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Natural experiment**: high ecological validity; behaviour observed in real-world settings | **Cannot fully control** all variables that changed in Notel over the 2 years alongside TV introduction |
| **Longitudinal design** tracks real change over time in the same community | **Attrition**: some children may have been lost to follow-up, affecting comparability |
| **Multiple measures** (observation, peer ratings, teacher ratings) provide method triangulation | The towns were not identical; pre-existing cultural or demographic differences may have influenced results |
| **Control communities** (Unitel, Multitel) provide a strong comparative baseline | Cannot determine **which types of TV content** drove the increase; general TV vs. specifically violent programming |
| Consistent with **Bandura (1961/63/65)** laboratory findings; supports SCLT in a naturalistic context | Researchers' coding of aggressive behaviour may have been subjective |

> **Key Concept - Natural Experiment:**
> *A natural experiment is a study where the independent variable occurs naturally, without manipulation by the researcher. Joy et al. (1986) is a textbook example: the "treatment" (TV introduction) happened independently of the researchers, who simply took advantage of the situation to measure its effects. This gives the study high ecological validity but limits experimental control.*
