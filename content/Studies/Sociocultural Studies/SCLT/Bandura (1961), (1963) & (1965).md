**Bandura** conducted a series of <u>Lab Experiments</u> on children demonstrating that **aggression can be learned through observation**, forming the empirical foundation for **Social Cognitive Learning Theory (SCLT)**.

> **Useful for:** *"Discuss social cognitive theory"* / *"Evaluate ethical considerations in psychological research"* / *"Discuss research methods in the study of the individual and the group"*

___

> [!Note]
> **Core Principle of SCLT:** Learning occurs not only through direct reward and punishment (behaviourism), but through **observation and vicarious reinforcement**, watching others being rewarded or punished for their behaviour.

___

**Aim:** To demonstrate that children who observe an adult model behaving aggressively towards a Bobo doll will imitate that aggression when given the opportunity, even without direct reward.
	Bandura predicted: 
	- Children will imitate the behaviour of the models
	- Children will imitate the behaviour of same-sex models to a greater degree

**Participants:** **72 children** (36 boys, 36 girls) from Stanford University Nursery School, mean age **52 months (~4.3 years)**. One male and one female adult model were used.

**Method:** <u>Lab experiment</u> with **independent groups design and matched pairs pre-testing**. Groups were pre-matched on aggression levels based on nursery school teacher evaluations, to control for pre-existing aggression as a confounding variable. **3 main IVs**:
1. Condition: aggressive model / non-aggressive model / no model (control)
2. Sex of the child
3. Sex of the model (same-sex vs. opposite-sex)

**Group structure (8 experimental groups + control):**

| | Aggressive Model | Non-Aggressive Model |
|---|---|---|
| Boys - same-sex model | 6 | 6 |
| Boys - opposite-sex model | 6 | 6 |
| Girls - same-sex model | 6 | 6 |
| Girls - opposite-sex model | 6 | 6 |
| **Control** (no model) | n/a | 24 |

Children were pre-tested to asses their aggressiveness. They were observed in a nursery and judge their behaviour on a 1-5 scale. Children were then match in each group so that they had similar levels of aggression

**Procedure - Three Stages:**

**Stage 1 - Model exposure (10 minutes):**
- Child was taken to the room and settled in their play area with "high-interest" items (potato prints, stickers).
- Adult model was escorted to the opposite corner with toys and a **Bobo doll**.
- **Non-aggressive condition**: Model played quietly with the blocks, ignoring the Bobo doll.
- **Aggressive condition**: After 1 minute, model turned to the Bobo doll and attacked it in a **scripted, specific way**: sitting on it, punching its nose, hitting it with a mallet, tossing it in the air, and using specific verbal phrases (*"Sock him in the nose!"*, *"Hit him down!"*, *"Pow!"*).

**Stage 2 - Mild aggression arousal:**
- Child was taken to another room with **very attractive toys**, but after beginning to play, was told: *"These are my best toys. I'm saving them for other children."*
- This was designed to create mild frustration, to increase the likelihood of aggression being expressed.

**Stage 3 - Test room (20 minutes):**
- Child was taken to a room containing both **aggressive toys** (Bobo doll, mallet, dart guns) and **non-aggressive toys** (tea set, crayons, toy cars).
- Behaviour was observed through a **one-way mirror** and coded by raters.

**Results:**
- Children in the **aggressive condition** showed **significantly more imitative aggression** than those in the non-aggressive or control conditions, both physical and verbal.
- **Boys imitated physical aggression more** than girls overall.
- **Same-sex model effect**: Boys imitated the male aggressive model more; girls showed more verbal aggression after observing the female model.
- Children also showed **non-imitative aggression**: novel aggressive acts not modelled, suggesting generalised disinhibition, not just copying.

**Conclusion:** Aggression can be **learned through observation** without direct reinforcement. Children are more likely to imitate same-sex models and male models (who show more physical aggression). The study supports SCLT's core claim that behaviour can be acquired through **observational learning and vicarious reinforcement**.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Controlled lab experiment**: high internal validity; clear manipulation of IVs | **Low ecological validity**: a Bobo doll is designed to be hit; children may not treat it as a real target |
| **Operationalized aggression** precisely: specific scripted acts, observed through one-way mirror | **Demand characteristics**: children may have perceived hitting Bobo as the expected behaviour |
| Results were **replicated** in the 1963 and 1965 follow-up studies | **Ethical concerns**: deliberately inducing aggression and frustration in young children |
| Foundational for **SCLT** and media violence research (Joy et al., 1986) | Does not measure long-term effects; laboratory aggression does not equal real-world violence |
| Influenced **media regulation, parenting theory, and educational practice** | Only measured **imitation** immediately after exposure; not long-term behaviour change |

___

## Follow-up Studies

| Study | Key Addition |
|---|---|
| **Bandura (1963)** | Compared live model vs. film vs. cartoon model; all produced similar aggression levels |
| **Bandura (1965)** | Added **vicarious reinforcement conditions**: children who saw the model punished showed less imitation, but equally when offered a reward for reproducing the acts, showing they had learned the behaviour but did not always perform it |

The 1965 study introduced the important distinction between **learning** (acquisition) and **performance**, a key refinement of SCLT.

> **Key Concept - Acquisition vs. Performance (Bandura, 1965):**
> *Bandura's 1965 follow-up showed that all children had learned the aggressive behaviour through observation, but only performed it when they expected a reward. This distinction between learning (cognitive acquisition of a behaviour) and performance (actually doing it) is crucial: it means exposure to media violence may teach aggressive scripts even if children do not immediately act aggressively.*
