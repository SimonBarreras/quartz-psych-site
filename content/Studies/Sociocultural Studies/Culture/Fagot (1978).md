#Study #Sociocultural [[Enculturation - Acquiring your cultural norms + Acculturation]], [[SCLT]]
___

**Fagot** conducted an <u>Overt Naturalistic Observation</u> examining how parents reinforce gender-appropriate behaviour in toddlers, demonstrating that cultural gender norms are transmitted through **direct tuition and channelling**, often without parents being consciously aware of their own behaviour.
___

**Aim:** To investigate the role of parents in gender-role development by observing parental reactions when a toddler's behaviour was or was not deemed gender-appropriate according to American cultural norms at the time.

**Participants:** **24 white American families**: 12 with a boy and 12 with a girl.
- Each family had only **one child**, aged **20-24 months**.
- Both parents lived at home and were aged **20-30 years old**.
- All families were white. Five lived in university housing, six in apartments, the rest in private homes.
- Sample varied in income; some parents were still students.

**Method:** <u>Overt naturalistic observation</u> with time sampling.
- Observers used a structured checklist of **46 child behaviours** and **19 parental reactions**.
- **Five 60-minute observations** were completed for each family over **five weeks*.
- The observer used **time sampling**, noting the child's behaviour every **60 seconds** and then recording the parent's response.
- **Two observers** were used to establish **inter-coder reliability**:
  - Agreement on child behaviour: **0.93**
  - Agreement on parental reactions: **0.83**
- After the observations, each parent was asked to rate the 46 behaviours as more appropriate for girls, for boys, or neutral.
- Each parent also completed a **questionnaire on the socialisation of sex roles**.

**Results:**
- Boys were more likely to be **left alone** by their parents than girls.
- Parents gave boys **more positive responses** when they played with blocks than they did girls.
- Parents gave girls **more positive responses** when they played with dolls or engaged in adult-oriented, dependent behaviour.
- Parents **criticized girls more** when they participated in large motor activities such as running and jumping.
- Parents gave **more positive responses to girls asking for help** and negative responses to boys doing the same.
- **Fathers** were more concerned with appropriate sex-typing behaviours for girls than for boys.
- Both parents identified more "girl only" appropriate behaviours than "boy only" behaviours.
- Parents reacted significantly **more favourably** when children engaged in gender-appropriate behaviour and were **more likely to give negative responses** to gender-inappropriate behaviour.
- **On the questionnaire**, parents did not report asking for help as a sex-preferred behaviour, yet their observed feedback clearly reinforced it as such, indicating that gender socialisation is largely **unconscious**.

**Conclusion:** Parents reinforce culturally defined gender norms through reward and punishment, often without being aware of doing so. The mismatch between observed behaviour and self-reported perceptions shows that gender socialisation operates unconsciously, an important finding for understanding how cultural norms are transmitted across generations.

**Link to enculturation:** Fagot (1978) directly demonstrates **direct tuition and channelling**: parents are transmitting culturally appropriate gender norms to their children through reinforcement, without intentionally teaching them. Children learn what is and is not appropriate behaviour in their culture through this unconscious parental feedback, making this a clear example of the enculturation of gender roles.

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **High ecological validity**: observations conducted in the natural family home, not a laboratory | **Sampling bias**: all families were white, American, and linked to a university setting; 24 families is a very small sample; findings cannot be easily generalized |
| **High inter-coder reliability** (0.93 and 0.83): two independent observers verified that recordings were not influenced by personal bias | **Demand characteristics**: families knew they were being observed; in reality there may be more or fewer behaviours that parents would normally reinforce or criticize |
| **Operationalized checklist** of 46 behaviours and 19 reactions allows systematic, replicable data collection | **Dated**: conducted in 1978 in the USA; gender norms have shifted considerably; findings may not reflect current parenting practices |
| **Method triangulation**: combines observation with parental self-report questionnaire, revealing the unconscious nature of gender socialisation | The observation checklist, while structured, still requires observer judgement about which behaviour category applies; some subjectivity remains |
| Provides strong evidence that gender socialisation is **unconscious**, a finding that self-report methods alone could not reveal | **Narrow cultural scope**: findings reflect American cultural gender norms of the 1970s; cross-cultural generalizability is very limited |

> **Key Concept - Unconscious Gender Socialisation:**
> *Fagot (1978) reveals that cultural transmission of gender norms does not require conscious intent. Parents who believed they treated boys and girls equally were, in practice, systematically reinforcing gender-typed behaviour through praise and criticism. This is enculturation through direct tuition and channelling: the culture teaches the child what is appropriate, and the parent is the unwitting vehicle of that transmission.*
