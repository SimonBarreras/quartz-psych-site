**Lueck and Wilson** conducted a <u>Survey Study using Semi-Structured Interviews</u> investigating which linguistic and social factors predict **acculturative stress** in Asian immigrants and Asian Americans, testing whether integration (maintaining both cultures) is more protective than assimilation.
___

**Aim:** To investigate which linguistic and social variables predict acculturative stress in a nationally representative sample of Asian immigrants and Asian Americans.

**Participants:** **2,095 Asian Americans** from the National Latino and Asian American Study (NLAAS).
- **1,271** were first-generation immigrants who arrived in the US aged 18 or older.
- The remainder were born in the US to first-generation immigrant parents.
- Multiple Asian cultures represented: approximately 600 Chinese, 508 Filipino, 520 Vietnamese, and 467 other Asians.

**Method:** <u>Semi-structured interviews</u>.
- Interviews conducted either **face-to-face or over the internet**.
- Interviewers were matched to participants by **cultural and linguistic background**.
- A randomly selected subsample was re-contacted to **validate interview data** (member checking).
- Variables measured included: acculturative stress, language proficiency, language preference, perceived discrimination, family cohesion, and socioeconomic status.

**Results:**
- **70%** of participants (1,433 of 2,095) showed acculturative stress.
- **Bilingual language preference** was associated with significantly **lower** acculturative stress.
- **English-only preference** was a predictor of **higher** acculturative stress.
- **Perceived discrimination** (prejudice, harassment, threats) was the **strongest predictor** of high stress.
- **Family cohesion** (shared values and beliefs) significantly **reduced** stress.
- **Economic satisfaction** and willingness to migrate again were associated with lower stress.

**Conclusion:** Acculturative stress is not reduced by assimilation. Maintaining bilingualism, cultural ties, and family cohesion are the strongest protective factors. This supports **integration over assimilation** as the healthier acculturation strategy, consistent with Berry's model.

---

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Large, diverse sample** (2,095 across four Asian cultural groups) increases reliability | Only studied **Asian Americans**: findings may not generalise to Latino, African, or European immigrant groups |
| **Culturally matched interviewers** increase comfort and reduce social desirability bias | **Semi-structured interviews** are time-consuming, costly, and open to **interviewer effects** |
| **Member checking** (validation subsample) strengthens credibility of data | **Constructs are subjective**: acculturative stress and economic satisfaction are difficult to measure objectively or compare across individuals |
| **Random sampling** from a national database reduces selection bias | **Ecological fallacy risk**: assuming all Asian cultures share collectivist values without measuring individual-level collectivism |
| Consistent with **Berry's integration model**; challenges the assumption that assimilation reduces stress | **Correlational**: cannot establish whether language preference causes stress levels or stress levels cause language preference |

> **Key Concept - Integration over Assimilation:**
> *Lueck & Wilson (2010) directly challenges the assumption that dropping one's original culture reduces stress. Bilingualism and cultural maintenance are protective; English-only assimilation is a risk factor. Discrimination is the single most harmful variable. Together these findings support Berry's integration strategy as the most psychologically healthy approach to acculturation.*
