#Study #Sociocultural [[Cultural dimensions]], [[Culture influence on behaviour + cognition]]
___

**Kearins** conducted a <u>Quasi-Experiment</u> comparing **visual-spatial memory** in Aboriginal Australian and white Australian children, providing direct evidence that **cultural background shapes cognitive strategies and memory performance**, and demonstrating that Western cognitive tests systematically underestimate the abilities of non-Western groups.
___

**Background:** Indigenous Australians have followed a semi-nomadic lifestyle of hunting and gathering for almost 60,000 years. Survival depended on finding routes through vast stretches of unmapped country and remembering locations of water, food, and wildlife, placing enormous demand on visual-spatial memory. Rainfall patterns in the desert are unreliable, so movement patterns were never duplicated year to year. Landmarks are few, so a general sense of spatial relationships was essential for navigation. This contrasts sharply with the verbal and textual demands of Western education. Indigenous Australians tend to score low on Western verbal intelligence tests, and Kearins tested whether they would outperform white Australians on a task that drew on visual-spatial rather than verbal abilities.

**Aim:** To investigate whether Aboriginal Australian children of desert regions would outperform white Australian children on a visual-spatial memory reconstruction task, based on the hypothesis that survival demands of desert life had shaped a culturally specific memory strategy.

**Participants:** Two matched groups of adolescents:
- **44 Aboriginal Australian children** from the Western Desert region of Australia, aged 12-16 (27 boys, 17 girls). Drawn from schools serving several widely separated desert settlements; raised by people living under semi-traditional tribal conditions. All spoke English as a second language.
- **44 white Australian children**, aged 12-16 (28 boys, 16 girls), drawn from a large high school in an outer suburb of Perth.

Groups were matched for age and sex ratio.

**Method:** <u>Quasi-experiment</u> (IV not manipulated; cultural background was a pre-existing condition).
- Kearins placed **20 objects on a board divided into 20 squares**. Children studied the board for **30 seconds** and were instructed to "look hard at all the things and try to remember where they all are." All objects were then heaped into a pile in the centre, and children were asked to reconstruct the original arrangement.
- No testing was done until children had had time and opportunity to ask questions. **Schoolrooms were not used; all work was done outdoors**, to reduce the cultural unfamiliarity of standardized testing for Aboriginal children.
- Qualitative observations of behaviour during the task were also recorded.
- Four task conditions:

| Condition | Objects | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Artificial-Different (A/D)** | 20 small man-made objects (knife, eraser, thimble, die, ring, scissors, matchbox), differing in colour, size, shape, and usage | Most familiar to white Australian children |
| **Natural-Different (N/D)** | 20 naturally occurring objects (feather, rock, bark, leaf, small skull, wildflower), differing from each other | Most familiar to Aboriginal children |
| **Artificial-Same (A/S)** | 12 small bottles arranged as a 4x3 matrix; differing in age, size, shape, and colour but not labelled and not commonplace, making verbal distinction difficult | Neither group specifically advantaged |
| **Natural-Same (N/S)** | 12 rocks differing in size, shape, colour, and texture | Neither group specifically advantaged |

**Results:**
- **Aboriginal children significantly outperformed white Australian children on all four conditions.**

| Condition | Level of significance |
|---|---|
| Artificial-Different (A/D) | p < .02 |
| Natural-Different (N/D) | p < .001 |
| Artificial-Same (A/S) | p < .001 |
| Natural-Same (N/S) | p < .001 |

- The least difference between groups was on the A/D condition (most familiar to white children), yet Aboriginal children still performed significantly better.
- Aboriginal children showed **no significant difference across conditions**: object type did not affect their performance, suggesting a generalized visual-spatial strategy rather than familiarity effects.
- White Australian children performed best with familiar, nameable objects, suggesting reliance on **verbal labelling strategies**. White children were observed to struggle with the A/S and N/S arrays, reporting difficulty knowing what to call the objects to distinguish them.

**Perfect score statistics:**
- **75%** of Aboriginal children made at least one perfect score; **41%** made two or more error-free performances.
- For white Australian children: perfect scores accounted for **less than 5%** of total performances; only **18%** managed one perfect score; **none** obtained two or more.
- For the 12-item arrays (A/S and N/S), over half of Aboriginal performances (45 of 88) were error-free.

**Qualitative observations:**
- Aboriginal children sat very still, did not engage in conversation, and reconstructed arrays with careful deliberation, steady progress, and few placement changes.
- White Australian children moved around on their seats, picked up and turned over objects, talked to themselves or the researcher, began reconstruction hurriedly, then slowed down, and made many changes toward the end.

**Conclusion:** Culture shapes the cognitive strategies used in memory tasks. Aboriginal children's desert environment had shaped superior visual-spatial memory encoded through direct visual perception rather than verbal labelling. Western intelligence tests, which rely heavily on verbal encoding, systematically underestimate non-Western cognitive abilities.

## Is this Enculturation?

The PDF source explicitly raises this question. The argument that Kearins demonstrates enculturation rests on the following:
- Indigenous Australian children learn **wayfinding techniques** from a young age by accompanying adults on journeys.
- Indigenous Australian languages use **cardinal directions** (N, S, E, W) rather than left/right, meaning speakers are continuously aware of directionality.
- Deep knowledge of the **night sky** is used for navigation, ceremonies, and storytelling, reinforcing spatial skills.
- **Songlines** encode detailed information about the landscape (routes, landmarks, water sources), functioning as mnemonic systems for spatial memory.

These cultural practices, transmitted across generations, explain how spatial memory is cultivated without requiring genetic explanation.

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Matched sample** (age, sex) controls for key demographic confounds | **Quasi-experimental**: findings are correlational; cannot establish that culture causally produced the memory difference |
| **Four task conditions** with exact significance levels (p < .001 for three conditions) strengthen the pattern of findings considerably | **Single cultural comparison**: replications with other indigenous groups are needed to establish reliability and generalizability |
| **Qualitative data** on behaviour provides rich insight into the different cognitive strategies each group used | The concept of standard testing is culturally foreign to Aboriginal children; despite outdoor testing, some performance suppression may have occurred |
| **Perfect score statistics** (75% of Aboriginal children vs. less than 5% of white children) demonstrate the magnitude of the difference strikingly | Cannot fully isolate whether superior performance reflects cultural transmission, ecological adaptation, or other variables such as nutrition or schooling |
| Has important **ethical and policy implications**: challenges the use of Western cognitive tests with non-Western populations | The researcher was white and Western; this power dynamic may have influenced participant behaviour |
| Kearins addresses and **dismisses the genetic explanation**: since these children were not raised in the desert themselves, the transmission is most likely cultural via their first-generation parents | The specific components of desert cultural life responsible for the memory advantage remain unclear |

> **Key Concept - Culture Shapes Cognition:**
> *Kearins (1981) demonstrates that cognitive strategies are not universal but shaped by ecological and cultural demands. The magnitude of the difference is striking: 75% of Aboriginal children achieved at least one perfect score, compared to fewer than 5% of white Australian performances. The study is also a methodological lesson: tests designed within one cultural framework systematically misrepresent the abilities of people from another. And because these children were not raised as desert hunters themselves, the most plausible explanation is cultural transmission through parenting, language, and practice, not genetics.*
