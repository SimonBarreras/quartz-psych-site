#PSY #Study #Sociocultural #Acculturation [[Enculturation - Acquiring your cultural norms + Acculturation]], [[Cultural dimensions]]
___

**Alexis Miranda and Kenneth Matheny** conducted a <u>Correlational Study using Questionnaires</u> investigating which socio-psychological factors predict **acculturative stress** in Latino immigrants to the United States, identifying both risk factors and protective factors that influence how well an individual adjusts to a new culture.
___

**Background:**
- **Acculturative stress** is the psychological and social difficulty experienced when an individual adapts to a new culture. It is linked to anxiety, depression, identity conflict, and reduced well-being.
- Berry's (1990) model argues that acculturative stress is shaped by multiple interacting factors, not a single cause. Miranda & Matheny directly tested this claim.
- **Protective factors** reduce acculturative stress; **risk factors** increase it. Identifying these factors has practical implications for mental health support services for immigrant populations.

**Aim:** To investigate which socio-psychological factors in the lives of Latino immigrants to the United States would predict and decrease the level of acculturative stress.

---

**Participants:** A **random sample of 197 Spanish-speaking Latino immigrants**, recruited from two social service agencies in the United States.

| Demographic | Detail |
|---|---|
| Gender | 63% female, 37% male |
| Mean age | 28.7 years |
| Mean age at migration | 22.5 years |
| Mean length of US residence | 3.9 years |
| Mexican ancestry | 47% |
| Central American | 34% |
| South American | 19% |

**Method:** <u>Correlational study</u> using self-report questionnaires and standardized psychometric tests.

Participants completed a battery of instruments measuring:

| Instrument | What it measured |
|---|---|
| **AIRS (American International Relations Scale)** | Level of acculturation (language use, cultural practices, social interactions) |
| **Family cohesion and adaptability scale** | Strength of family structure and closeness |
| **Acculturative stress scale** | Level of psychological stress related to cultural adaptation |
| **Coping resources effectiveness scale** | Quality and efficacy of strategies used to manage stress |
| **Demographic questionnaire** | Age, gender, nationality, length of US residence, language proficiency |

---

**Results:**
- Five predictor variables together explained acculturative stress in Latino immigrants:
  1. **Coping resources**: Immigrants with effective coping strategies showed significantly less acculturative stress. Insufficient coping was the **single largest predictor** of acculturative stress.
  2. **Degree of acculturation**: Higher acculturation (greater adoption of US cultural norms and language) was associated with lower stress.
  3. **Language use**: Greater English proficiency and use was associated with lower acculturative stress. Spanish-only speakers showed significantly higher stress.
  4. **Family cohesion**: Immigrants with stronger, more cohesive family structures experienced less stress.
  5. **Length of residence**: Longer time in the US was associated with lower acculturative stress and higher acculturation levels.

**Conclusion:** Acculturative stress is not caused by a single factor but is shaped by multiple interacting socio-psychological variables. The findings support Berry's (1990) assertion that acculturative stress is multifactorial. Protective factors (coping strategies, English proficiency, family cohesion, longer residence) buffer individuals against stress, while their absence constitutes risk. The study suggests that the closer an immigrant moves toward **integration** (engaging with the new culture while retaining family and identity), the less stress they experience.

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Random sampling** from social service agencies increases the representativeness of the sample within this population | **Correlational design**: cannot establish cause-and-effect; acculturative stress may influence family cohesion rather than the reverse |
| **Large sample** (n=197) for a study of this type; includes multiple Latin American nationalities, widening scope | **Self-report questionnaires**: vulnerable to social desirability bias; participants may underreport stress or overreport family closeness |
| **Multiple standardized instruments** used; triangulation of measures increases construct validity | **WEIRD-adjacent limitation**: recruited from social service agencies, so participants may already be more distressed or more engaged with support systems than the general immigrant population |
| **Gender-balanced** sample (63% female, 37% male); findings are not androcentric | Results may not generalize beyond Latino immigrants in the US; different cultural groups face different stressors |
| Supports and extends **Berry's (1990) multifactorial model** of acculturative stress | **No quantitative data** reported for the length-of-residence finding, reducing precision |
| Findings have direct **clinical and policy applications**: identifying protective factors allows targeted interventions | Recruited from only **two social service agencies**, limiting geographic and demographic diversity within the Latino immigrant population |

## Comparison with Lueck & Wilson (2010)

Miranda & Matheny and Lueck & Wilson are the two core acculturation studies for IB. They are frequently used together and can be directly compared:

| Feature | Miranda & Matheny (2000) | Lueck & Wilson (2010) |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | Latino immigrants | Asian Americans (first and second generation) |
| **n** | 197 | 2,095 |
| **Method** | Questionnaire / correlational | Semi-structured interview / correlational |
| **Key protective factors** | Coping strategies, English proficiency, family cohesion, length of residence | Bilingual language preference, family cohesion, no discrimination, economic satisfaction |
| **Key risk factor** | Insufficient coping resources | Perceived discrimination |
| **Supports Berry?** | Yes | Yes |
| **Sampling** | Random (2 agencies) | Random (national database) |

Both studies confirm that acculturative stress is multifactorial and that **integration** (engaging with the new culture while maintaining original identity and family ties) is more protective than assimilation. The key difference is that Lueck & Wilson explicitly finds that **dropping the native language increases stress**, while Miranda & Matheny finds that **gaining English reduces stress** -- these are not contradictory but complementary: bilingualism is the optimal state.

---

> **Key Concept - Protective Factors in Acculturation:**
> *Miranda & Matheny (2000) operationalizes Berry's theoretical claim that acculturative stress is multifactorial. Rather than asking whether acculturation causes stress, it asks what makes stress more or less likely. The answer is that coping resources, language competence, family support, and time all interact to either buffer or amplify the difficulty of cultural adaptation. This shifts the clinical focus from the immigrant as a problem to the absence of support structures as the problem.*
