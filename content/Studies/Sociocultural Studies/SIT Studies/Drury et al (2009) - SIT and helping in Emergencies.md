#Study #VR #SIT [[Ethical considerations]], [[Social Identity Theory]]
___

**John Drury et al.** conducted a <u>Lab Experiment using Virtual Reality</u> investigating whether **shared social identity** increases prosocial (helping) behaviour during a simulated emergency, overcoming the ethical barriers of studying real disasters.
___

**Background - Levine et al. (2005):**
A related study tested in-group bias on helping with 45 male Manchester United fans. A confederate fell and cried out in pain wearing either a Manchester United shirt, a Liverpool FC shirt, or a plain shirt. Participants were most likely to help the Manchester United fan and least likely to help the Liverpool or plain-shirt wearer, showing that in-group membership, not general goodwill, drives helping.

**Aim:** To investigate the role of social identity and group categorization in prosocial (helping) behaviour during a simulated emergency.

**Participants:** **40 University of Sussex students**, aged 20-25.

**Method:** <u>Lab experiment using VR</u> with independent groups design.
- **Priming**: Participants read a report about a deadly fire and imagined sensory details to increase psychological realism.
- **Scenario**: Participants were presented with a VR simulation of a fire emergency in the London Underground and had to escape while deciding whether to help or push others.
- **Group identification condition**: Participants were told they were part of a group of England football supporters returning from a match.
- **Individual identification condition**: Participants were told they were just individuals returning home.
- The size of the crowd in the VR simulation was either 8 or 32 people (this had no significant effect on results).

**Results:**
- Participants in the **group identification condition** provided significantly **more help** to others and **pushed fewer people** than those in the individual identification condition.
- A shared social identity increased the likelihood of prosocial behaviour in an emergency: when people perceive themselves as part of a collective, they are more likely to act for the benefit of the group rather than just themselves.

**Conclusion:** Group identity plays a significant role in promoting prosocial behaviour during emergencies. Social categorization shapes whether people help or prioritize self-preservation.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Ethically sound**: VR allowed researchers to study a high-stakes emergency without putting participants in real danger | **Highly artificial**: participants are aware they are in a simulation; genuine fight-or-flight panic cannot be replicated |
| **High internal validity**: controlled VR environment allows researchers to isolate social identity as the cause of helping behaviour | **Demand characteristics**: participants may have guessed the aim after reading the fire report, or treated the simulation as a game |
| **Mundane realism**: VR provides a more immersive experience than text-based scenarios, leading to more authentic behavioural data | **Generalizability**: sample limited to University of Sussex students; their behaviour may not represent how different demographics would react |
| Supports **Social Identity Theory** in a prosocial context | Crowd size manipulation had no effect, which was unexpected and limits interpretation |

> **Key Concept - Social Identity and Helping:**
> *Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979) predicts that we are more likely to help members of our in-group because their welfare is tied to our own social identity. Drury et al. (2009) confirms this in an emergency context: shared group identity transforms a crowd of individuals into a collective that looks out for each other, with significant implications for emergency management and evacuation planning.*
