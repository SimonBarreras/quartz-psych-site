#Study #SIT [[Ethical considerations]], [[Social Identity Theory]]
___

**Muzafer Sherif et al.** conducted a <u>Field Experiment</u> investigating how **intergroup conflict** develops between groups and whether it can be reduced through **superordinate goals**, cooperative tasks requiring intergroup collaboration to achieve a shared outcome.
___

**Aim:** To investigate (1) whether intergroup conflict would naturally emerge between two groups of boys given competitive conditions, and (2) whether superordinate goals could reduce that conflict.

**Participants:** **22 white, middle-class, Protestant boys aged 11-12** from Oklahoma City, USA. All were strangers to each other, psychologically well-adjusted, and matched across groups for ability and background. Parents were not told the full nature of the study.

**Method:** <u>Field experiment</u> at Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma, a summer camp setting.

**Three phases:**

**Phase 1 - In-group formation (1 week):**
- The boys were divided into **two groups** (later named the Eagles and the Rattlers), kept completely separate.
- Each group bonded through cooperative activities: hiking, camping, swimming.
- Both groups developed **group norms, hierarchies, and names** spontaneously.

**Phase 2 - Intergroup conflict (1 week):**
- The two groups were brought into **direct competition** through a tournament (baseball, tug-of-war, etc.), with prizes only for the winning group.
- Researchers observed the development of:
  - **In-group favouritism**: strong loyalty and positive stereotypes of their own group
  - **Out-group hostility**: name-calling, raiding each other's cabins, burning the rival's flag
  - Clear **derogatory stereotyping** of the out-group ("sneaky," "cheats")

**Phase 3 - Conflict reduction (1 week):**
- **Contact alone** (eating together, watching films) did NOT reduce hostility; it actually increased tension.
- Only **superordinate goals**, tasks requiring the two groups to work together (e.g., fixing the camp's water supply, pulling a stalled truck), successfully reduced conflict and led to cross-group friendships.

**Results:**
- Intergroup competition reliably produced **hostility, stereotyping, and discrimination** between the groups.
- **Superordinate goals** progressively reduced hostility and led to spontaneous intergroup friendship by the end of Phase 3.
- Supports the view that conflict arises from **competition over resources** (Realistic Conflict Theory), not pre-existing prejudice.

**Conclusion:** Intergroup conflict can develop rapidly between groups placed in competition, even without prior hostility. It can be reduced not through simple contact but through **cooperation toward shared goals**, with real-world implications for reducing prejudice and ethnic conflict.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **High ecological validity**: real camp setting; behaviour was natural and spontaneous | **Serious ethical concerns**: boys were deceived; aggression was deliberately induced without full parental consent |
| **Rich qualitative data**: detailed observations of real social dynamics | **Sampling bias**: all-white, middle-class, Protestant boys from one city; very low generalizability |
| Clear demonstration of both **conflict formation and reduction** in the same study | Researchers intervened at multiple points; difficult to isolate a single IV |
| Supports **Realistic Conflict Theory** and **Social Identity Theory** | The study has been criticized for being staged; Sherif may have interfered more than reported |
| Longitudinal field design tracks real behavioural change over time | Cannot replicate exactly; unique setting, specific sample |

> **Key Concept - Superordinate Goals:**
> *A superordinate goal is an objective that is compelling for both groups but cannot be achieved by either group alone, requiring cooperation. Sherif et al. showed that contact alone does not reduce prejudice (and may increase it), but working together toward a shared goal does. This has been applied to school desegregation programmes (e.g., the Jigsaw Classroom technique).*
