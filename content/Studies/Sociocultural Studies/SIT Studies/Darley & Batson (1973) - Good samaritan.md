#Study #SIT [[Ethical considerations]], [[Experiments]]
___

**John Darley and Daniel Batson** conducted a <u>Field Experiment</u> investigating whether **situational factors** (specifically time pressure) rather than **dispositional factors** (such as religiosity or moral concern) determine whether people help someone in need.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether the degree of hurry a person is in (situational variable) affects helping behaviour, and whether the topic of a speech they had just prepared (moral priming) has any effect, testing situational vs. dispositional explanations of prosocial behaviour.

**Participants:** **40 seminary students** (students training to be ministers) at Princeton Theological Seminary, all confirmed to be religious, controlling for dispositional religiosity.

**Method:** <u>Field experiment</u> with independent groups design.

**Two independent variables:**
1. **Speech topic** (dispositional prime):
   - Half prepared a speech on the **Good Samaritan** (Biblical parable about helping)
   - Half prepared a speech on **job opportunities for seminary graduates**
2. **Degree of hurry** (situational variable):
   - **Low hurry**: "You have plenty of time, head over now."
   - **Medium hurry**: "You're right on time, head over now."
   - **High hurry**: "You're late, they're expecting you."

**Procedure:**
- Participants were sent across campus to deliver their speech in another building.
- Along the route, they encountered a **confederate slumped in a doorway**, coughing and groaning, appearing to be in distress.
- Whether the participant **stopped to help** (and the quality of help) was recorded as the DV.

**Results:**

| Condition | % Who Helped |
|---|---|
| **Low hurry** | **63%** |
| **Medium hurry** | **45%** |
| **High hurry** | **10%** |

- **Speech topic had no significant effect**: even those who had just prepared the Good Samaritan speech often walked past without helping when in a hurry.
- Some participants in the high-hurry condition **literally stepped over** the confederate.

**Conclusion:** **Situational factors**, specifically time pressure, were the dominant predictor of helping behaviour, overriding dispositional factors like religiosity and even moral priming. This challenges the assumption that prosocial behaviour is driven primarily by personality or values.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **High ecological validity**: real campus setting, real confederate in distress | **Ethical concerns**: confederate feigned distress; participants were deceived and not fully debriefed until later |
| **Controlled variables** (all seminarians, same confederate, same location) increase internal validity | **Sample**: seminary students are not representative of the general population |
| Clearly demonstrates the **power of situation** over moral disposition | Participants may have noticed the same person on different occasions, creating potential awareness issues |
| Results are **counterintuitive** and compelling; even Good Samaritan speech-givers often did not help | Only measures a **binary helping decision**; does not capture quality or nature of help offered |
| Consistent with **Zimbardo's** findings on situational power over behaviour | Cannot fully rule out demand characteristics in some conditions |

> **Key Concept - Situational vs. Dispositional Explanations:**
> *Dispositional explanations attribute behaviour to internal characteristics (personality, values, beliefs). Situational explanations attribute behaviour to external context (time pressure, social norms, environment). Darley & Batson (1973) powerfully demonstrates that even people with strong prosocial values will fail to help when situational demands are pressing, a profound challenge to moral consistency assumptions.*
