#Study #HRelations #Sociocultural [[Ethical considerations]], [[Social Identity Theory]]
___

**Irving Piliavin, Judith Rodin and Jane Piliavin** conducted a <u>Field Experiment</u> investigating helping behaviour on the New York City subway, examining how **situational factors** such as type of victim, race, and the presence of a model influence whether bystanders intervene. The study directly challenged the **diffusion of responsibility** hypothesis.
___

**Background:** Following Darley & Latané's laboratory demonstrations of the bystander effect, Piliavin et al. moved research into a naturalistic setting to test whether diffusion of responsibility would hold in a face-to-face, unavoidable emergency. They proposed the **Arousal: Cost-Reward Model** as an alternative explanation for helping behaviour.

**Aim:** To investigate the effect of type of victim (ill vs. drunk), race of victim, and presence of a model on the speed and frequency of helping behaviour in a real-world setting.

**Participants:** Approximately **4,450 passengers** on New York City subway trains travelling between Harlem and the Bronx on weekdays between 11:00am and 3:00pm. Opportunity sample; approximately 45% Black, 55% white. Around 43 passengers per carriage, with approximately 8.5 in the critical area near the victim.

**Method:** <u>Field experiment</u> with independent groups design.
- Four research teams of 4 confederates each (2 male, 2 female) conducted **103 trials** on trains that did not stop between two stations, giving a 7.5-minute journey window.
- After approximately 70 seconds into the journey, the **male confederate victim** collapsed and lay on the floor.
- **Independent variables:**
  - **Type of victim**: either carrying a **cane** (ill, appearing physically disabled) or smelling of alcohol and carrying a bottle in a paper bag (**drunk**)
  - **Race of victim**: Black or white confederate
  - **Model behaviour**: whether a second confederate helped early (70 seconds) or late (150 seconds), and whether they stood in the critical or adjacent area
- **Dependent variables**: time taken for a passenger to help, total number of helpers, race and sex of helpers, verbal comments made.
- Two female confederates recorded observations from seats in the carriage.

**Results:**
- **Cane condition**: spontaneous help given on **95%** of trials (62/65); median time to help: 5 seconds.
- **Drunk condition**: spontaneous help given on **50%** of trials (19/38); slower response.
- **No diffusion of responsibility effect**: group size did not significantly reduce helping in the cane condition; more bystanders sometimes meant more help, not less.
- **Race effects**: in the cane condition, race of victim had little effect. In the drunk condition, there was more same-race helping.
- **Modelling**: when a model helped early, others were more likely to follow.
- As time passed without help in the drunk condition, some bystanders moved away from the area.

**Conclusion:** Helping behaviour in face-to-face emergencies is primarily determined by the **costs and rewards** of helping, not by diffusion of responsibility. An ill victim (low cost of helping, high social approval) receives near-universal help. A drunk victim (higher cost: possible violence, vomiting, embarrassment) receives less. Piliavin et al. proposed the **Arousal: Cost-Reward Model**: witnessing an emergency creates arousal which motivates action, and the chosen response depends on a rapid cost-benefit calculation.

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **High ecological validity**: real subway, real bystanders, genuine unscripted behaviour | **Serious ethical concerns**: no informed consent, no right to withdraw, no debriefing; participants may have experienced distress from witnessing or failing to help |
| **Large sample** (~4,450 passengers) increases representativeness within the NYC subway population | **Cannot control extraneous variables**: passenger density, temperature, prior experiences on the journey |
| **Inter-rater reliability**: two independent observers cross-checked data | **Limited generalizability**: NYC subway passengers are not representative of all populations; results may differ in rural, less urban settings |
| Directly challenges **diffusion of responsibility** theory in a real-world setting | Some participants may have encountered the same trial more than once if they used that route regularly |
| Provides the **Arousal: Cost-Reward Model** as a theoretically grounded alternative explanation | Deceit was inherent to the design; ethical standards today would prevent this study from being run |

> **Key Concept - Arousal: Cost-Reward Model:**
> *Piliavin et al. proposed that witnessing an emergency generates psychological arousal (discomfort, distress). This arousal motivates the bystander to act, but the specific action chosen depends on a rapid mental calculation: what are the costs and rewards of helping vs. not helping? A cane victim presents low costs (physically manageable, socially approved) and high rewards (reduced arousal, social approval), producing near-universal help. A drunk victim presents higher costs (possible violence, embarrassment), tipping the calculation toward inaction or avoidance. Crucially, this model predicts that diffusion of responsibility will be weaker in face-to-face settings where the costs of inaction (sustained arousal, moral discomfort) are also high.*
