#Study #SIT [[Role-Based Behaviour]], [[Ethical considerations]]
___

**Philip Zimbardo** conducted a <u>Simulation Study</u> investigating how **social roles** and **situational factors** influence behaviour, demonstrating that ordinary people can rapidly adopt extreme behaviours when placed in a role-defined social structure.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether participants would conform to the social roles of prisoner and guard in a simulated prison environment, and whether situational rather than dispositional factors would drive their behaviour.

**Participants:** **24 male college students** (emotionally stable, psychologically healthy, screened via personality tests), randomly assigned to roles:
- **Guards** (11 participants): Given uniforms, mirrored sunglasses, batons, and told to maintain order.
- **Prisoners** (10 participants): "Arrested" at home by real police, processed, given smocks and numbers, deindividuated.
- A small number were alternates who were not used.

**Method:** <u>Simulation/role-playing study</u>, not a true experiment (no independent variable was systematically manipulated).
- A mock prison was set up in the **basement of Stanford University's psychology department**.
- The study was planned to last **2 weeks**.
- Zimbardo himself took the role of **Prison Superintendent**, which has been criticized for compromising his objectivity.

**Results:**
- Within **days**, guards became increasingly **authoritarian, dehumanizing, and abusive**: forcing prisoners to do push-ups, denying toilet privileges, staging humiliating roll-calls at night.
- Prisoners became **passive, distressed, and depressed**; 3 were released early due to severe emotional breakdown.
- Participants appeared to **fully internalize their roles**, losing sight of their real identities.
- The study was **stopped after 6 days** (not 2 weeks) when Zimbardo's colleague Christina Maslach observed the conditions and objected strongly.

**Conclusion:** Situational factors and assigned roles can powerfully shape behaviour, sometimes overriding personal values. The situation rather than individual personality largely determined how both guards and prisoners behaved.

___

> **Later Reinterpretation - SIT Perspective (Reicher & Haslam, 2006):**
> The BBC Prison Study (2001) replicated a similar scenario and found that groups did **not** inevitably become abusive; behaviour depended on **group identity and leadership**. Reicher & Haslam argued that Zimbardo's results reflected **social identity dynamics** (guards actively identifying with their role and enforcing it) rather than automatic role conformity. This suggests the situation does not determine behaviour mechanically: **group identity and active identification** are key mediators.

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **High mundane realism**: participants appeared to genuinely internalize their roles | **Serious ethical violations**: no right to withdraw was honoured; psychological harm was caused; deception was used |
| Demonstrates the **power of situation** over disposition | Zimbardo played the role of Superintendent, creating a **lack of objectivity**; he may have encouraged guard aggression |
| Highly **influential**: shaped thinking on obedience, prisons, and dehumanization | **Cannot generalize**: volunteer sample, artificial setting, American college men only |
| Relevant to understanding **prison reform, torture, and war atrocities** | Not a true experiment; no control group; many confounding variables |
| Supported by real-world parallels (Abu Ghraib) | Recent **reanalysis** (Le Texier, 2018) revealed Zimbardo coached guards to act tough, undermining the "spontaneous" claim |

> **Key Concept - Situational vs. Dispositional Attribution:**
> *Zimbardo's study challenges the fundamental attribution error, the tendency to explain others' behaviour through personality rather than situation. His finding that ordinary, psychologically healthy students became abusive guards suggests that the situation (role, uniform, power structure) was the primary driver of behaviour. However, more recent critiques show the picture is more complex: guards were coached, and social identity (active identification with a role) matters more than passive role assignment.*
