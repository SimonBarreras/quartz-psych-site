#Study #SIT [[Minimal Group Paradigm]], [[Social Identity Theory]]
___

**Tajfel** conducted a <u>Lab Experiment</u> demonstrating that **social categorization alone**, with no history of conflict, competition, or personal interaction, is sufficient to produce **in-group favouritism and out-group discrimination**. This became the foundation for **Social Identity Theory (SIT)**.
___

**Aim:** To investigate whether a minimally significant group is sufficient to produce discrimination against an out-group and favouritism toward an in-group.

**Participants:** **48 schoolboys aged 14-15** from a school in Bristol, UK, all of whom knew each other before the study.

**Method:** <u>Lab experiment</u> using the **Minimal Group Paradigm**.

**Procedure:**
1. **Categorization**: Boys were shown paintings by Kandinsky and Klee and asked which they preferred. They were then told they had been assigned to a group based on their preference (Klee group or Kandinsky group). **They did not know which artist painted which work**, making the grouping essentially meaningless. (Minimal group paradigm)

2. **Point allocation task**: Each boy was given a booklet of matrices and asked to allocate points to pairs of other boys, one from his group and one from the other. They were never allocating points to themselves.

3. **Three allocation strategies** were available:
   - **Maximum Joint Profit (MJP)**: Give both boys as many points as possible
   - **Maximum In-Group Profit (MIP)**: Give the in-group member as many points as possible regardless of the out-group's score
   - **Maximum Differentiation (MD)**: Give the in-group member more points than the out-group member, even if this means sacrificing overall points for both

**Results:**
- Boys rarely chose **Maximum Joint Profit**; they did not try to maximize overall points.
- The dominant strategy was **Maximum Differentiation**: boys sacrificed total points in order to ensure their in-group member received relatively more than the out-group member.
- This occurred even though the groups were **meaningless, temporary, and anonymous**.

**Conclusion:** The **Minimal Group Paradigm** demonstrates that social categorization alone is sufficient to produce discrimination. People do not need real conflict, history, or personal stakes to favor their in-group over an out-group; mere awareness of group membership triggers in-group favoritism. This is the empirical basis for **Social Identity Theory**.

___

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **High internal validity**: controlled lab setting isolates social categorization as the sole IV | **Low ecological validity**: artificial task (point matrices) does not reflect real-world discrimination |
| **Replicable**: the MGP has been replicated with many different populations and group criteria | **Demand characteristics**: boys may have treated the task as a competition or game ("screw-you effect") |
| Foundational for **Social Identity Theory** (Tajfel & Turner, 1979) | Sample of **teenage boys from one school**; low generalizability to other ages, cultures, genders |
| Shows discrimination emerges from **categorization**, not competition or prejudice | Cannot explain why categorization leads to favouritism; SIT offers the theoretical explanation |
| Elegant minimal design; clearly demonstrates the phenomenon with few confounds | The point allocation system was complex; some boys may not have fully understood the trade-offs |

> **Key Concept - Social Identity Theory (Tajfel & Turner, 1979):**
> *SIT proposes that part of our self-concept (social identity) is derived from our membership in social groups. To maintain positive social identity, we favour our in-group and derogate out-groups, not because of real conflicts, but simply because group membership matters to our sense of self. Tajfel (1971) provides the empirical foundation: even trivial, arbitrary group membership triggers this tendency.*
