#Study #SIT [[Conformity]], [[Social Identity Theory]]
___

This note covers both **Asch's original conformity paradigm (1951)** and **Abrams et al.'s (1990)** extension, which introduced **Social Identity Theory** as an explanation for when and why conformity occurs.

___

# Part 1: Asch (1951) - The Line Judgment Paradigm

**Aim:** To investigate the extent to which social pressure from a majority group influences an individual to conform, even when the correct answer is obvious.

**Participants:** **50 male college students** who believed they were participating in a "vision test."

**Method:** <u>Lab experiment</u>.
- Participants were placed in groups of **7-9 people**, of whom all but **one were confederates**.
- They were shown a standard line and asked to match it to one of three comparison lines; the correct answer was always obvious.
- After several correct trials, confederates began giving **unanimous incorrect answers**.
- The real participant always answered **second to last**, hearing the majority's wrong answer before giving their own.

**Results:**
- On critical trials, **32% of responses** were conforming (incorrect).
- **75% of participants conformed at least once** across 18 critical trials.
- **25% never conformed** at all.
- In a control condition (no confederates), error rate was less than **1%**.

**Conclusion:** Social pressure from a unanimous majority causes a significant proportion of people to give answers they know to be wrong, demonstrating **normative social influence** (conforming to be accepted) and possibly **informational social influence** (doubting one's own judgement).

___

# Part 2: Abrams et al. (1990) - SIT Extension of Asch

**Aim:** To investigate whether **social identity** (in-group vs. out-group status of the confederates) moderates conformity, as predicted by Social Identity Theory.

**Participants:** Psychology students at the University of Dundee.

**Method:** <u>Lab experiment</u> with 2x2 independent groups design.

**Two IVs:**
1. **Group membership of confederates**: In-group (fellow psychology students) vs. out-group (ancient history students)
2. **Visibility of response**: Public vs. private

**Results:**
- **77%** conformed at least once overall.
- Conformity was **highest in the in-group public condition**: participants conformed most when the majority were fellow in-group members and their response was visible.
- Conformity was **lowest in the out-group condition**: participants resisted influence from out-group members.
- There was **no significant difference** between out-group public and private conditions, suggesting conformity to out-groups is avoided regardless of visibility.

**Conclusion:** Conformity is not simply about majority pressure; it is moderated by **social identity**. People conform to in-group norms to maintain group membership and social identity, but resist influence from out-groups. This supports **SIT's** claim that group membership shapes social behaviour.

___

## Evaluation (Abrams et al.)

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Extends Asch** by adding a theoretically motivated IV (in-group vs. out-group) | **Lab setting**: artificial task; conformity in real social contexts may differ |
| Provides **empirical support for SIT** in a controlled experimental design | **Sample**: psychology students from one university; low generalizability |
| **Standardized procedure** with high replicability | The in-group/out-group manipulation may not have been equally salient for all participants |
| Clearly demonstrates that conformity is **socially motivated**, not just perceptual | Only tests conformity to majority; does not address minority influence |
| Consistent with Tajfel & Turner's (1979) theoretical framework | Results may reflect **demand characteristics** in the public conditions |

> **Key Concept - Normative vs. Informational Social Influence:**
> *Normative influence: conforming to be liked and accepted, even while privately disagreeing. Informational influence: conforming because you genuinely doubt your own judgement and believe the group may be right. Asch's paradigm produces primarily normative influence; participants knew the group was wrong but went along anyway. Abrams et al. shows that this effect is strongest with in-group members, because acceptance by the in-group matters more to social identity.*
