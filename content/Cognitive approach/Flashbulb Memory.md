## Definition

A **flashbulb memory (FBM)** is a highly detailed, exceptionally vivid memory of the moment and circumstances in which surprising and personally consequential news was heard. The term was coined by **Brown & Kulik (1977)**.

FBMs are a type of **autobiographical memory**. They feel permanent and accurate, but research consistently shows that this confidence is not matched by actual accuracy.

---

## Brown & Kulik (1977) - Original Theory

Brown & Kulik proposed that FBMs are produced by a **special biological memory mechanism** they called **Now Print**, triggered when an event meets a threshold of surprise and emotional significance. They identified six characteristic features of FBMs:

- **Place**: where you were when you heard the news
- **Ongoing event**: what you were doing
- **Informant**: who told you
- **Own affect**: your emotional reaction
- **Other affect**: the emotional reaction of others present
- **Aftermath**: what happened immediately after

They proposed that **rehearsal** (repeatedly thinking or talking about the event) strengthens and maintains FBMs over time. The more personally consequential the event, the more likely a FBM is to form.

**Evaluation of the theory:**
- It assumes FBMs are a qualitatively special type of memory with higher accuracy — this is challenged by Neisser & Harsch (1992).
- The "Now Print" mechanism has never been directly demonstrated neurobiologically.
- The model does not fully account for why vividness and confidence persist even when the memory is inaccurate.

---

## Are FBMs Actually Accurate? The Core Debate

The central question is whether FBMs are **genuinely more accurate** than ordinary memories, or simply **more vivid and more confidently held**.

**Brown & Kulik (1977)** argued yes, the special mechanism produces reliable, accurate memories.

**Neisser & Harsch (1992)** challenged this directly. Using the Challenger disaster as the trigger event, they showed that FBMs are subject to significant distortion over 2.5 years, with a mean accuracy score of only 2.95/7, while participants maintained high confidence (avg. 4.17/5). Critically, when shown their original accounts, most participants could not explain the discrepancies. See: [[Neisser & Harsch (1992) - Space Challenger Disaster]].

**Talarico & Rubin (2003)** followed up with 54 Duke University students after 9/11 and found that flashbulb memories and everyday memories both declined in accuracy at the same rate over time. The only difference was that participants retained greater **belief in the accuracy** of their FBMs, vividness and confidence diverged from actual accuracy.

**Sharot et al. (2007)** provides a partial defence of the FBM concept from a biological angle. Using fMRI, they found that participants who were physically closer to Ground Zero on 9/11 showed stronger **amygdala activation** when recalling the event and reported more vivid, confident memories. This suggests personal proximity and emotional arousal contribute to the formation of stronger (though not necessarily more accurate) memory traces. See: [[Sharot et al (2007) - 911]].

---

## Biological Basis

The neurobiology of FBMs centres on the interaction between the **amygdala** and **hippocampus** during emotionally arousing events.

- The amygdala processes emotional significance and modulates memory consolidation in the hippocampus.
- High emotional arousal triggers the release of **adrenaline and cortisol**, which enhance encoding of the event.
- This is consistent with **McGaugh & Cahill's** (1995) work showing that blocking adrenaline (via propranolol) impairs memory for emotional material.
- Sharot et al. (2007) found amygdala activation correlated with FBM vividness, providing direct neuroimaging support.

However, enhanced neural encoding does not guarantee accuracy,  the brain may encode a vivid but partially reconstructed version of an event.

---

## Why Do FBMs Feel Accurate Despite Being Distorted?

The most important insight from FBM research is the **dissociation between confidence and accuracy**. Several explanations:

- **Reconstructive memory** (Bartlett, 1932): all memory is reconstructive, built from schemas and post-event information. FBMs are not exempt.
- **Rehearsal effects**: repeatedly retelling the memory consolidates the later (distorted) version, not the original. Exposure to media coverage of an event further contaminates the original trace.
- **Metacognitive failure**: the vividness of a FBM is experienced as evidence of its accuracy, but vividness reflects arousal at encoding, not fidelity of storage.

## IB Exam Framing

For the question *"To what extent is one cognitive process reliable?"* using memory/FBM:
- Define FBM and Brown & Kulik's claims.
- Use Neisser & Harsch (1992) as the primary counter-evidence (accuracy vs. confidence dissociation).
- Use Sharot et al. (2007) to introduce biological nuance (amygdala, proximity).
- Conclude that FBMs are not qualitatively special in terms of accuracy, but are distinctive in terms of vividness, confidence, and neural correlates.

For *"Discuss the role of emotion in one cognitive process"*:
- Use Sharot et al. (2007) for biological evidence of emotion enhancing encoding.
- Use Neisser & Harsch (1992) to show emotion does not protect against distortion.
- Use rehearsal and reconstructive memory to explain the confidence-accuracy gap.
