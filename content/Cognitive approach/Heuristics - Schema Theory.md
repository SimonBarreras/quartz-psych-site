This note covers two closely related cognitive concepts: **Schema Theory** and **Heuristics**. Both explain how the brain processes information efficiently, and both have implications for memory, decision-making, and the reliability of cognitive processes.

---

## Schema Theory

### What is a Schema?

A **schema** is a mental representation of knowledge derived from prior experience, stored in the brain to help organize information, guide behaviour, assist recall, and make sense of new experiences.

Schemas are not passive storage systems. Humans are **active information processors**: the brain constantly integrates new incoming information (bottom-up, from the senses) with existing knowledge (top-down, from schemas). When information is missing, the brain fills in the gaps based on what schemas predict should be there, which frequently leads to distortions and errors.

> **Key functions of schemas:**
> - Speed up perception, memory, and decision-making
> - Help predict what to expect based on past experience
> - Allow the brain to handle complexity without processing everything from scratch

---

### Types of Schemas

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Social schemas** | Knowledge about the world, groups, and social situations | Expectations about how a doctor's appointment will go |
| **Self-schemas** | Knowledge and beliefs about oneself | "I am good at maths" |
| **Scripts** | Learned sequences of behaviour appropriate to specific situations | Knowing how to behave in a restaurant without being told |

**Scripts** are particularly important: they are patterns of behaviour learned through interaction with the environment and are developed within a **cultural context**, meaning they are not universal across societies.

---

### How Schemas are Created and Updated

| Process | What happens | When it occurs |
|---|---|---|
| **Assimilation** | New information is absorbed into an existing schema without changing it | When new information fits well with what we already know |
| **Accommodation** | A new schema is created or an existing one is significantly restructured | When new information cannot fit into any existing schema |

---

### Schemas and Memory

Schemas have a direct influence on what we remember and how accurately we remember it. Because memory is reconstructive (Bartlett, 1932), schemas act as a scaffold during recall: we remember the gist and fill in the rest based on what our schema tells us should have been there.

This produces predictable distortions:
- **Schema-congruent** information (fitting the schema) is more likely to be remembered, but also more likely to be falsely recalled when it was not actually present.
- **Schema-incongruent** information (not fitting the schema) is more likely to be forgotten or distorted to fit.
- Schemas can lead to **over-generalisations**, including stereotypes, when assumptions based on prior experience are applied too broadly to new people or situations.

**Linked studies:**
- [[Bartlett (1932) - War of Ghosts]]: Cultural schemas led British participants to distort an unfamiliar Native American folk tale through levelling, sharpening, and rationalisation.
- [[Brewer & Treyers (1981)]]: Participants recalled schema-congruent objects from an office as present even when they were not, while schema-incongruent objects were more likely to be forgotten or distorted.
- [[Loftus & Palmer (1974) - Car collision]]: Post-event information (the verb used in a question) activated a schema of a more severe accident, leading to false memory of broken glass.

## Heuristics

### What is a Heuristic?

A **heuristic** is a mental shortcut: a simple rule or strategy that focuses on one aspect of a complex problem and ignores others, allowing faster judgements with less cognitive effort.

Heuristics are a type of schema-based processing. Rather than analysing all available information systematically (System 2 thinking), the brain defaults to fast, pattern-matching shortcuts (System 1 thinking).

---

### Why We Use Heuristics: Cognitive Miserliness

Humans are **cognitive misers**: we tend to avoid deep processing when it is not strictly necessary, in order to conserve cognitive resources. This tendency arises from three factors:

- **Knowledge**: we already have schemas that can provide a quick answer
- **Motivation**: we often lack the desire to invest effort in a thorough analysis
- **Economy**: deep processing is slow and cognitively expensive

The result is a trade-off: heuristics allow faster, more efficient thinking, but at the cost of accuracy and susceptibility to systematic errors (cognitive biases).

---

### Key Heuristics

**Anchoring bias** (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974): the tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of numerical information encountered (the anchor) when making subsequent judgements, even when the anchor is arbitrary or irrelevant.

**Representativeness heuristic** (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983): judging the probability of something by how closely it resembles a prototype or stereotype, ignoring statistical base rates. Produces the conjunction fallacy.

**Availability heuristic**: estimating the likelihood of an event based on how easily examples come to mind, which tends to overweight dramatic or recent events.

**Illusory correlation** (Hamilton & Gifford, 1976): perceiving a relationship between two variables (e.g., minority group membership and negative behaviour) when no such relationship exists, because both are distinctive and thus cognitively salient.

---

### Mnemonics

Mnemonics are a positive application of heuristic processing: they use schemas and associations to encode information more efficiently.

Common techniques include:
- **Music, rhythm, and rhyme**: exploiting the phonological loop and pattern recognition
- **Acronyms and chunking**: grouping information into fewer, more memorable units (cf. Miller's Magic Number 7)
- **Method of loci**: associating information with specific locations in a mentally visualised space (e.g., the "memory palace" technique)

---

> **Key Concept - The Overlap between Schemas and Heuristics:**
> *Both schemas and heuristics reflect the same underlying principle: the brain prioritises speed and efficiency over accuracy. Schemas provide ready-made frameworks for interpreting new information; heuristics provide ready-made rules for making decisions. Both are highly adaptive in most everyday situations, but both produce predictable, systematic errors when the shortcuts do not match the demands of the task. This is why the study of cognitive biases is, at its core, the study of what happens when efficient mental shortcuts go wrong.*
