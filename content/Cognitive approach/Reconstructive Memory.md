**Reconstructive memory** is the theory that memories are not stored as complete, fixed recordings in the brain. Instead, every time we remember something, we actively **reconstruct** it from fragments of stored information, filling in gaps using schemas, expectations, and post-event information. This process makes memory flexible and efficient, but also fundamentally unreliable.

The concept was first systematically demonstrated by **Bartlett (1932)**, who argued that memory is an active, meaning-making process rather than a passive playback system.

---

## Core Principle

When we encode an experience, we do not store every detail. We store a **gist** shaped by our existing schemas. When we later recall it, we reconstruct the event by combining what was actually stored with what our schemas tell us should have been there. This means:

- Memory is always influenced by **prior knowledge** (schemas)
- Memory can be altered by **post-event information** (misinformation effect)
- Memory can be influenced by the **way questions are asked** (leading questions)
- Entirely **false memories** can be created through suggestion

---

## Key Processes and Phenomena

### Schema-Driven Distortion

Schemas act as a scaffold during recall. Information that fits our schemas is more likely to be remembered (and falsely recalled if absent), while schema-incongruent information is more likely to be forgotten or distorted to fit.

Bartlett (1932) identified three specific distortion processes in serial reproduction of an unfamiliar story:

| Process | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Levelling** | The memory becomes shorter; unfamiliar or incongruent details are omitted |
| **Sharpening** | Certain details are emphasised and become more prominent over time |
| **Rationalisation** | Unfamiliar or supernatural elements are reinterpreted to fit existing cultural schemas |

### Post-Event Misinformation

Information encountered after an event can be incorporated into the memory of that event without the person noticing. This is known as the **misinformation effect**.

Loftus & Palmer (1974) demonstrated that changing a single verb in a post-event question ("smashed" vs. "contacted") altered both speed estimates and created false memories of broken glass that was never present in the original film.

### False Memory Implantation

Entirely fabricated events can be implanted as genuine memories through repeated suggestion over time. Loftus & Pickrell (1995) showed that 25% of participants came to "remember" a childhood event (getting lost in a shopping mall) that had been confirmed by family members to have never occurred.

Shaw (2015) extended this further, showing that false memories of having committed a crime can be implanted in healthy adults through repeated interview techniques.

### Confidence vs. Accuracy

A critical finding across reconstructive memory research is that **confidence does not predict accuracy**. People can hold vivid, highly confident memories that are substantially or entirely wrong.

Neisser & Harsch (1992) found that fewer than 10% of participants gave fully accurate accounts of how they heard about the Challenger disaster 2.5 years after the event, yet participants showed high confidence in their incorrect memories and sometimes refused to accept their original written accounts as genuine.

### Weapons Focus / Tunnel Vision

When a threatening object such as a weapon is present during an event, attention narrows to that object, reducing encoding of peripheral details. This is sometimes called the **weapons focus effect** and is a specific form of the broader influence of emotional arousal and cognitive load on encoding.

### Attentional Blindness

When cognitive resources are consumed by one task (high cognitive load), people fail to notice other events entirely, even prominent ones. This limits what gets encoded in the first place, creating gaps that reconstruction later fills.

## Linked Studies

| Study | Key contribution |
|---|---|
| [[Bartlett (1932) - War of Ghosts]] | First systematic demonstration of schema-driven distortion; levelling, sharpening, rationalisation |
| [[Loftus & Palmer (1974) - Car collision]] | Post-event verbal information alters speed estimates and creates false memories |
| [[Loftus & Pickrell (1995) - Lost in a mall]] | Entirely false autobiographical memories can be implanted through suggestion |
| [[Neisser & Harsch (1992) - Space Challenger Disaster]] | Flashbulb memories are not reliably accurate despite high confidence |
| [[Brewer & Treyers (1981)]] | Schema-congruent objects falsely recalled; schema-incongruent objects forgotten or distorted |
| [[Yuille & Cutshall (1986) - Gun shop robbery]] | Real-life eyewitness memories can be highly accurate; challenges lab-based findings |

## Strengths and Limitations of the Reconstructive Memory Framework

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| Supported by a large and consistent body of experimental research | Much of the evidence comes from artificial lab tasks; real-world memory may be more robust (cf. Yuille & Cutshall) |
| Has significant real-world applications: eyewitness testimony, therapy, police interviews | Does not fully explain why some memories (especially for highly emotional events) are highly accurate |
| Explains why memory errors are **systematic** rather than random | The role of individual differences in susceptibility to false memories is underexplored |
| Challenges the intuitive view of memory as a recording device | Ethical concerns around research that deliberately implants false memories or distresses participants |

> **Key Concept - Reconstructive Memory:**
> *Memory is not a camera. It is a reconstruction: every recall is an active process of piecing together stored fragments with schema-based inferences. This is why two people who experience the same event remember it differently, why leading questions alter testimony, and why entirely false memories feel just as real as genuine ones. The reliability problem is not a flaw in the system; it is an inherent consequence of how memory is designed to work.*
