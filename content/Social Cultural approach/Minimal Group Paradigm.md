___

The **minimal group paradigm** is a psychological research method used to investigate the minimal conditions required for discrimination to occur between groups. It was developed by **Henri Tajfel** in the 1970s as part of his work on social identity theory.

Even **arbitrary, meaningless group distinctions** (like being told you’re in “Group A” or “Group B” based on a coin toss or preference for abstract art) are enough for people to show **in-group favoritism** and **out-group discrimination**.

___

**How it Works:**

1. **Participants** are randomly assigned to groups (e.g., based on trivial choices like preferring Kandinsky or Klee paintings).

2. They are **not told who else is in their group** and **do not interact with other participants**.

3. They are then asked to assign **rewards or points** to anonymous members of the two groups.

4. **Results consistently show** that people give more points or resources to their own group, even though group membership is arbitrary and has no real meaning.

___

**Key Findings:**

• **Group identification** happens quickly—even with minimal or meaningless group criteria.

• People **favor their own group**, even if it means the other group gets significantly less.

• This behavior forms the foundation of **in-group bias**, which plays a role in stereotyping, prejudice, and intergroup conflict
