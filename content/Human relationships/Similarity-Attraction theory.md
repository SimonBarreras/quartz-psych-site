#PSY #HRelations #Theory [[Human Relationships]]
___

**Similarity-Attraction Theory** proposes that people are attracted to others who are similar to themselves in attitudes, values, personality, and background. The greater the degree of similarity, the greater the interpersonal attraction. The theory was developed primarily by **Donn Byrne (1971)** through experimental research using the "bogus stranger" paradigm.

___

## Core Claims

- **Attitude similarity** is the primary driver of attraction. We are drawn to those who share our views because it validates our own worldview (reinforcement model).
- Similarity reduces uncertainty and potential conflict, making interaction feel rewarding.
- The relationship between similarity and attraction is roughly **linear**: more similarity predicts more attraction.
- This applies across multiple domains: attitudes, values, personality traits, physical attractiveness level, and demographic background.

---

## Key Supporting Studies

### Byrne (1971) - Bogus Stranger Paradigm

**Aim:** To test whether attitude similarity causes increased attraction to a stranger.

**Method:** Laboratory experiment. Participants completed an attitude questionnaire. They were then given a questionnaire supposedly completed by a stranger, which was manipulated to show either high (75%) or low (25%) agreement with the participant's own attitudes. Participants rated the stranger on attraction and likeability scales.

**Results:** Attraction ratings were significantly higher when the stranger's attitudes closely matched the participant's own. The effect was consistent across studies and replicated many times.

**Conclusion:** Attitude similarity directly causes increased attraction, independent of any real interaction.

---

### Markey & Markey (2007) - see [[Markey & Markey (2007)]]

Extends the theory to real couples. Found that people describe their ideal partner in terms closely matching their own personality, and that similar couples report greater relationship harmony. However, some complementarity in dominance-related traits was associated with the best outcomes, qualifying the purely linear claim of the original theory.

---

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Substantial empirical support**: the bogus stranger paradigm has been replicated across many cultures and attitude domains | **Bogus stranger paradigm lacks ecological validity**: rating a paper stranger is not the same as real relationship formation |
| Explains a **consistent real-world pattern**: couples tend to share similar values, backgrounds, and attitudes (matched hypothesis) | **Attitude similarity may be a consequence of attraction**, not a cause: couples grow more similar over time, making causation unclear |
| Supported by real couple data (Markey & Markey, 2007): similar couples are more harmonious | **Complementarity also matters**: Markey & Markey (2007) found that some opposite traits (dominance) predict better outcomes, challenging a purely similarity-based model |
| Accounts for why homogamy (marriage within similar social groups) is universal across cultures | **Overemphasis on cognitive factors**: ignores biological factors such as MHC-based mate selection (Wedekind, 1995), physical attractiveness, and evolutionary drives |
| Useful in explaining **long-term relationship stability**, not just initial attraction | Theory does not explain why people are sometimes attracted to dissimilar others or why initial attraction based on similarity can fade (see Felmlee, 1995 - Fatal Attraction) |

> **Key Concept - Similarity-Attraction Theory:**
> *Byrne's (1971) Similarity-Attraction Theory proposes that attitude similarity directly causes interpersonal attraction, because sharing beliefs and values with another person is reinforcing: it validates our own worldview and reduces conflict. The more similar two people are, the more attracted they will be to each other. While well-supported by experimental evidence, the theory has been criticised for its artificial methodology and for underestimating the role of complementarity and biological factors in real relationship formation.*
