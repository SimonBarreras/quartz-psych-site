A **stereotype** is an exaggerated oversimplification about an individual based on their group membership or physical features. It is a generalization, positive or negative, made about a group and then attributed to individuals within it. Stereotypes are a specific type of **schema**: a mental framework that organises and simplifies social information.

---

## Sources of Stereotypes

Campbell (1967) identified two key sources:

- **Personal experience**: direct interactions with members of a group, which may be overgeneralised
- **Gatekeepers**: parents, media, school, and other cultural agents who transmit stereotyped beliefs through enculturation

---

## Formation of Stereotypes

Stereotypes can be explained at multiple levels of analysis.

---

### Cognitive Explanation: Illusory Correlation

As **cognitive misers**, humans use mental shortcuts called **heuristics** to simplify complex information and minimise cognitive effort. One such heuristic is the **illusory correlation**: the tendency to perceive a relationship between two variables when no such relationship actually exists.

When a minority group and a negative behaviour are both statistically rare, their occasional co-occurrence is disproportionately salient and memorable. This leads people to overestimate how often minority group members engage in negative behaviour, producing a negative stereotype with no basis in actual group differences.

**Key study: Hamilton & Gifford (1976)**
- 40 undergraduate participants read 39 behavioural statements about hypothetical Group A (26 members, majority) and Group B (13 members, minority).
- The ratio of desirable to undesirable behaviours was identical for both groups (18:8 for A; 9:4 for B), so there was no real difference.
- Participants significantly overestimated the number of undesirable behaviours associated with Group B and rated them more negatively.
- Conclusion: stereotypes can form through purely cognitive mechanisms, without prejudice, conflict, or real group differences, purely because minority membership and negative behaviour are both distinctive.

**Other heuristics involved in stereotype formation:**
- **Availability heuristic**: estimating the likelihood of something based on how easily examples come to mind; dramatic or memorable events involving out-group members are overweighted
- **Representativeness heuristic**: judging individuals by how closely they resemble a group prototype
- **Confirmation bias**: selectively attending to information that confirms existing beliefs while ignoring disconfirming evidence

---

### Sociocultural Explanation: Social Identity Theory

**Social Identity Theory** (Tajfel & Turner, 1979) proposes that people categorise themselves and others into social groups. To maintain a positive social identity, people engage in **positive distinctiveness**: favouring the in-group and derogating the out-group. This process of **social categorisation** produces overgeneralised, often negative beliefs about out-groups.

**Key study: Rogers & Frantz (1961)**
- Investigated prejudice and stereotyping in Rhodesian whites toward Black Rhodesians.
- Found that the longer white Europeans had lived in Rhodesia, the more prejudiced they became.
- Supports a **conformity and SIT explanation**: exposure to the dominant cultural group increased adoption of in-group stereotypes through conformity to social norms.
- Challenges purely cognitive accounts: stereotypes are also actively reinforced through social belonging and cultural conformity.

---

### Biological Underpinning

Stereotypes have neurological correlates that reflect their basis in schema and heuristic processing:

- The **amygdala** is activated by out-group faces, producing a rapid threat-detection response before conscious evaluation occurs
- Stereotypes are represented as **neural networks**: frequently activated associations become stronger through synaptic reinforcement (**neuroplasticity**)
- Priming a stereotype activates its associated neural network, making related information more cognitively available (consistent with the availability heuristic)

---

### Learning Explanation

Stereotypes can also be acquired through **classical conditioning** (Pavlov, 1927): repeated pairing of a neutral stimulus (e.g., a social group) with a negatively valenced unconditioned stimulus produces a conditioned negative response to the group. This explains how stereotypes can be transmitted through media, where groups are repeatedly shown in negative contexts.

---

## Effects of Stereotypes

### Stereotype Threat

**Stereotype threat** occurs when awareness of a negative stereotype about one's group impairs performance on tasks relevant to that stereotype. The mechanism: threat creates anxiety which consumes cognitive resources (working memory), reducing performance.

**Spotlight anxiety**: the heightened self-consciousness of being a visible member of a stereotyped group in a relevant performance context.

**Key study: Steele & Aronson (1995)**
- 114 Black and white Stanford students completed a difficult verbal test.
- In the stereotype threat condition (framed as a test of intellectual ability), Black students performed significantly worse than white students of equivalent SAT scores.
- In the non-threat condition (framed as a laboratory problem-solving task), the performance gap virtually disappeared.
- Conclusion: mere awareness of a stereotype is sufficient to impair performance, even without the individual believing the stereotype.

**Spencer et al. (1999)** extended this to gender: women underperformed on maths tests when told men typically do better, but not when no such framing was given.

---

### Memory Distortion

Stereotypes shape what we remember and how we remember it, through schema-congruent processing:

- **Martin & Halverson (1983)**: children who were shown gender-inconsistent images (e.g., a girl playing with a gun) later misremembered them as gender-consistent (a boy playing with a gun), demonstrating schema-driven memory distortion
- **Allport & Postman (1947)**: in a serial reproduction task, participants misremembered a White man holding a razor as a Black man holding it, showing racial stereotypes can distort memory encoding
- **Payne (2001)**: priming participants with Black faces led them to misidentify harmless objects as weapons faster, demonstrating unconscious stereotype activation affecting perception

## Prejudice and Discrimination

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Stereotype** | A generalised belief (positive or negative) about the characteristics of a social group, attributed to individuals within it |
| **Prejudice** | A negative attitude toward members of a group, based on stereotyped beliefs; an evaluative judgement |
| **Discrimination** | Behaviour that treats members of a group differently based on their group membership; the behavioural expression of prejudice |

Prejudice and discrimination do not always co-occur. A person may hold prejudiced beliefs without discriminating (e.g., due to social norms), or may discriminate without conscious prejudice (e.g., Fagot, 1978).

> **Key Concept - Stereotype as Schema:**
> *Stereotypes function as social schemas: mental frameworks that simplify the processing of information about groups. Like all schemas, they speed up social judgements but at the cost of accuracy. They are reinforced neurologically through repeated activation, which is why they persist even when individuals consciously reject them. The cognitive miser framework explains why stereotypes are so difficult to eliminate: effortful System 2 thinking is required to override them, and humans default to System 1 shortcuts under cognitive load.*
