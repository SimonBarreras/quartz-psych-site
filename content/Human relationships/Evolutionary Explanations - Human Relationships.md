#PSY #HRelations #Biological
___

Evolutionary psychology applies the theory of **natural selection** to human behaviour, arguing that the behaviours and preferences we exhibit today exist because they maximised reproductive success in our evolutionary past. In the context of human relationships, this means attraction, mate selection, and relationship behaviour are shaped by what helped our ancestors survive and reproduce.

> **Core claim:** Behaviours that increased reproductive success were passed on. Behaviours that reduced it were selected against. Human attraction is therefore not random but follows predictable, evolved patterns.

---

## Key Theoretical Framework

### Natural Selection and Mate Choice

**Natural selection** favours traits that maximise an organism's reproductive success. In the context of attraction, this means:
- We are attracted to cues that signal **genetic fitness, good health, and reproductive viability**
- We have evolved **psychological mechanisms** for detecting these cues, including sensitivity to physical features, immune compatibility, and genetic diversity

### Parental Investment Theory (Trivers, 1972)

The sex that invests more biologically in offspring (gestation, nursing) will be more **selective** in mate choice. The sex that invests less will seek to **maximise the number of mating opportunities**.

In humans:
- **Women** invest more (9-month pregnancy, nursing, primary caregiving) and are therefore more selective, prioritising resources, stability, and genetic quality.
- **Men** invest less biologically and therefore tend to be less selective and more focused on cues to fertility (youth, physical attractiveness).

This predicts **universal sex differences** in mate preferences across cultures.

## Key Studies

### Buss (1989) - Universal Mate Preferences

**Aim:** To investigate whether sex differences in mate preferences are universal across cultures, supporting an evolutionary explanation.

**Participants:** Over 10,000 participants from 37 cultures across 6 continents.

**Method:** Cross-cultural self-report survey. Participants rated 18 characteristics desired in a long-term mate.

**Results:**
- Women consistently valued financial resources, ambition, and older age more than men across nearly all 37 cultures.
- Men consistently valued physical attractiveness and youth more than women.
- Men preferred partners approximately 2.5 years younger; women preferred partners approximately 3.5 years older.

**Conclusion:** The universality of sex differences supports Parental Investment Theory: women select for resource-holding capacity; men select for fertility cues. Cultural variation in specifics, but the pattern is universal.

---

### Fisher et al. (2005) - Love as a Motivational Drive

Romantic love activates the **dopaminergic reward pathway** (VTA, caudate nucleus), the same system involved in motivation and goal pursuit across species. Fisher argues this system evolved to focus mating energy on one specific partner, increasing reproductive success by ensuring sustained investment. See [[Fisher et al 2005]] for full details.

---

### Wedekind (1995) - MHC and Immune-Based Mate Selection

Women prefer the body odour of MHC-dissimilar men, supporting an evolved mechanism for maximising offspring immune diversity. The preference reverses in women taking oral contraceptives, suggesting a direct hormonal link to evolved mate detection systems. See [[Wedekind (1995)]] for full details.

---

### Clark & Hatfield (1989) - Sex Differences in Short-Term Mating

A field experiment on a US university campus showed 75% of men vs. 0% of women agreed to a casual sexual proposition from an attractive stranger. This directly demonstrates the sex difference in short-term mating receptivity predicted by Parental Investment Theory.

---

### Ronay & von Hippel (2010) - Testosterone and Mate Display

96 male skateboarders took significantly more risks in the presence of an attractive female observer, with saliva testosterone levels rising in that condition. This demonstrates evolved **intrasexual competition and mate display** behaviour with a hormonal mechanism.

## The Biochemical Basis of Attraction

The PDF source identifies a **"love cocktail"** of neurotransmitters and hormones involved in attraction:

| Substance | Role in attraction |
|---|---|
| **Dopamine** | Motivation and reward; drives goal-directed pursuit of a specific partner |
| **Noradrenaline** | Alertness and arousal; the racing heart and sweating palms of early attraction |
| **Serotonin** | Drops in early love; associated with obsessive thinking about the partner (similar to OCD levels, Marazziti et al., 1999) |
| **Testosterone** | Increases sexual desire and motivates active pursuit of a partner |
| **Oxytocin** | Released during touch and sex; deepens bonding and attachment in established relationships; suppresses amygdala activity, reducing anxiety |
| **Vasopressin** | Linked to long-term pair bonding and mate guarding (Winslow et al., 1993 in prairie voles; Walum et al., 2008 in human males) |

## Evaluation

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Cross-cultural universality** (Buss, 37 cultures) provides strong support for evolved preferences | Much research is **correlational**: cannot establish that evolutionary pressures caused the observed preferences |
| **Biological evidence** (Fisher's fMRI, Wedekind's MHC, Ronay's testosterone) grounds evolutionary claims in measurable mechanisms | **Reductionist**: reduces complex human attraction to biochemistry and genes, ignoring cognitive and sociocultural factors |
| Parental Investment Theory generates **clear, testable predictions** that have been confirmed cross-culturally | **Animal generalisability**: findings from prairie voles and mice are used to infer human behaviour; the leap is large |
| Explains **sex differences** in mating that are consistent across very different cultures | **Sociocultural alternative** (Eagly & Wood, 1999): sex differences in mate preference may reflect social roles and economic inequality rather than evolved psychology |
| Consistent with findings from multiple methods: survey (Buss), fMRI (Fisher), field experiment (Clark & Hatfield), naturalistic (Ronay) | The **contraceptive reversal** (Wedekind) shows that modern technologies can disrupt evolved preferences, raising questions about how adaptive these mechanisms still are |
