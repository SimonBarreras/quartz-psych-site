**Bowlby's Attachment Theory** is a foundational biological and cognitive framework explaining how early emotional bonds between infants and caregivers form, why they matter for survival, and how they shape behaviour across the lifespan, including adult romantic relationships.

---

## Core Idea

Attachment is the development of a **mutual and intense emotional relationship** between an infant and its caregivers. Bowlby argued that a close relationship between child and caregiver is a **basic biological need**, an innate pattern of behaviour shaped by evolution to promote infant survival.

Infant behaviours such as **smiling, babbling, grasping, and crying** are genetically-based social signals that encourage caregivers to provide **food, protection, and affection**.

---

## Development of Attachment

According to Shaffer (1996), attachment develops until around **age 7 months**, at which point:
- The infant shows **separation anxiety** when the primary attachment figure leaves.
- The infant can discriminate between the attachment figure and strangers (linked to Piaget's **object permanence**).
- The infant displays **stranger anxiety** when unfamiliar people approach.

---

## Harlow's Challenge to Freud

Freud's **cupboard theory** argued the mother-child bond forms because the mother provides nourishment. **Harry Harlow (1958)** challenged this using rhesus monkeys given both a wire mother (providing milk) and a cloth mother (providing comfort). Monkeys spent significantly more time with the **cloth mother** regardless of which provided food, demonstrating that **contact comfort**, not feeding, is the basis of attachment.

---

## The Internal Working Model

Bowlby (1973) argued there is a **continuity between early attachment and adult relationships**. Children create an **Internal Working Model (IWM)**, a cognitive schema of:
- What to expect from attachment figures
- Ideas about the self (am I lovable?)
- How the self and others relate

The IWM guides behaviour in **conflict resolution, self-disclosure, and relationship evaluation** throughout life.

- Children who experience **love and affection** come to see themselves as worthy of love.
- Children who experience **neglect or rejection** may come to believe they deserve it, negatively affecting mental health and future relationships.

---

## Ainsworth's Strange Situation

Mary Ainsworth (1970) devised the **Strange Situation Paradigm** to measure attachment behaviour based on how a child reacts to maternal separation and reunion. She conducted the **Ganda Project** (Uganda: 28 children, 9 months) before replicating it in Baltimore (26 mother-infant pairs).

**Attachment Types:**

| Type | Name | Behaviour | % of children |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Insecurely attached - Avoidant | Apparent indifference when mother leaves; avoids contact on return; not afraid of strangers | 20% |
| **B** | Securely attached | Upset when mother leaves; happy on reunion; easily comforted | 70% |
| **C** | Insecurely attached - Anxious-Ambivalent | Very upset when mother leaves; difficult to soothe; seeks then rejects comfort on return | 10% |
| **D** | Insecurely attached - Disorganised *(Main & Solomon, 1986)* | No consistent reaction; associated with abuse or chronic depression in caregiver | - |

**Evaluation of the Strange Situation:**
- Highly standardised; allows replication across time and cultures.
- Critics argue it is highly **artificial** and gathers limited information.
- **Van IJzendoorn & Kroonenberg (1988)** meta-analysed 32 studies (8 countries, 2000+ infants) and found cultural differences in attachment distribution. Japanese studies showed no Type A but high Type C, associated with different child-rearing practices.
- The **meaning of separation differs across cultures**, challenging universal interpretation.

---

## Role of Early Attachment on Later Behaviour

Bowlby's **continuity hypothesis** proposes that internal working models continue throughout the lifespan, though they can change.

**Supporting research:**
- **Hazan & Shaver (1987)**: childhood attachment style predicts adult romantic relationship style (secure, avoidant, ambivalent).
- **Simpson et al. (1996)**: insecurely attached individuals used more negative conflict strategies in dating couples.
- **Fonagy et al. (1991)**: prospective longitudinal study (n=96 London mothers) found a 75% match between a mother's attachment style and her child's security, supporting **intergenerational transmission** of attachment.

---

## Evaluation of Attachment Theory

| Strengths | Limitations |
|---|---|
| **Cross-cultural support** for basic attachment behaviours (proximity-seeking, separation anxiety) | **Cross-cultural differences** in attachment distribution challenge universality claims |
| Strong empirical base (Ainsworth, Hazan & Shaver, Fonagy et al.) | **Deterministic risk**: theory can overstate the permanence of early attachment patterns |
| IWM provides a **testable cognitive mechanism** linking early experience to adult behaviour | IWM is **difficult to measure directly**; relies on inferences from behaviour |
| Explains both **healthy and problematic** adult relationship patterns | Ignores other influences on adult relationships (e.g., peer relationships, culture, socioeconomic factors) |
| Foundation for **therapeutic interventions** (e.g., attachment-based therapy) | Overemphasis on the **mother-infant dyad**; underestimates the role of fathers and other caregivers |

> **Key Concept - The Internal Working Model:**
> *The IWM is the cognitive bridge between childhood attachment and adult relationships. It is not a rigid template but a working model, meaning it can be updated by new experiences. A child with an anxious attachment history may develop a secure style through a later positive relationship or therapy. Bowlby's most important contribution is not determinism, but the insight that our earliest relationships teach us what to expect from intimacy, and that these lessons echo throughout our lives.*
