Cultural influences on behaviour + cognition

Cultural influences on behaviour + cognition

May 04, 20265 min read

  • PSY
  • Sociocultural

PSY Sociocultural Cultural Dimensions, Berry (1967), Kearins (1981), Kulkofsky et al (2011), Hofstede (2001)

Culture is not just a backdrop to human behaviour — it actively shapes how people think, remember, perceive, and act. The sociocultural approach investigates how group membership, cultural norms, and shared values influence individual psychology.

What is culture? A dynamic system of rules, explicit and implicit, established by groups in order to ensure their survival. It involves attitudes, values, norms, and behaviours shared by members of a group across generations.

Two Approaches to Studying Culture

Before studying cultural influences, it is important to understand the methodological approaches researchers use, as this affects what conclusions can be drawn.

Etic ApproachEmic Approach
DefinitionStudies culture from the outside, using standardized tools applied across culturesStudies culture from within, immersing in that culture to understand it on its own terms
GoalCross-cultural comparisonDeep cultural understanding
MethodsExperiments, surveys, standardized tests, factor analysisObservation, interviews, case studies, culturally specific tools
StrengthsComparable, quantifiable, replicableRich insight, culturally valid, context-sensitive
LimitationsMay impose Western concepts on non-Western cultures; risk of ecological fallacyNot generalizable; time-intensive; researcher may go native
ExampleHofstede (2001): same survey applied across 40 countriesBolton (2001): immersive case study of cross-cultural validity of DSM diagnosis

The ecological fallacy (Hofstede’s warning): even when two cultures differ on average, you cannot assume that any two individuals from those cultures will differ, or that every member of a culture will demonstrate the cultural norm.

How Culture Influences Behaviour

Conformity

Berry (1967) demonstrated that cultural demands shape conformity. Using an adapted Asch line-matching task with three groups (approximately 120 participants each): the Temne of Sierra Leone (collectivist, farming society), the Inuit of Baffin Island (individualist, hunting society), and Scottish people as a Western reference group.

  • Temne participants conformed significantly more to incorrect group answers than either Inuit or Scottish participants.
  • Inuit participants conformed even less than the Scottish group.
  • No significant difference within groups based on degree of Western exposure, suggesting that cultural identity itself drives conformity, not current lifestyle.

This is based on Barry, Child & Bacon (1959), which proposed that hunting societies tend toward individualism while agricultural societies tend toward collectivism, due to the different survival demands of each.

Memory and Cognition

Spatial memory (Kearins, 1981): Aboriginal Australian children significantly outperformed white Australian children on a visual-spatial object reconstruction task across all four object conditions. Aboriginal children used direct visual encoding strategies; white children relied on verbal labelling. This shows that the cognitive strategies used in memory are not universal but are shaped by ecological and cultural demands. Crucially, Western-style cognitive tests underestimate the abilities of non-Western groups by measuring only the cognitive strategies valued in Western education.

Flashbulb memory (Kulkofsky et al., 2011): A cross-cultural study of 274 middle-class adults from five countries (USA, Germany, UK as individualist; China, Turkey as collectivist) found that:

  • Individualist cultures developed FBMs for a wider range of events, including international ones, placing emphasis on personal involvement and emotional response.
  • Collectivist cultures developed stronger FBMs for events of national or social significance, de-emphasizing individual emotional experience.
  • Social sharing was the strongest predictor of FBM strength across all cultures: the more an event was discussed with others, the more vivid and durable the memory. This effect was amplified in collectivist cultures where communal discussion is a social norm.

Cross-Cultural Validity of Diagnosis

Bolton (2001) used an emic approach, a case study with ICA (inductive content analysis) with 40 local participants, to investigate the cross-cultural validity of DSM diagnostic categories. His findings suggest that Western diagnostic frameworks may not translate accurately to non-Western cultural contexts, raising important questions about the ethnocentrism embedded in psychological classification systems.

Key Concepts for Exams

Enculturation: The process by which individuals learn and internalize the norms, values, and behaviours of their own culture. It is largely implicit and begins at birth through observation and participation in cultural practices.

Acculturation: The process of psychological and cultural change that occurs when individuals from one culture come into sustained contact with another. Berry (2005) identifies four strategies: integration, assimilation, separation, and marginalisation. Research (Lueck & Wilson, 2010) shows integration, maintaining both the original culture and adapting to the new one, produces the least acculturative stress.

Emic vs. etic: The emic approach yields culturally specific insight but cannot be generalized. The etic approach allows comparison but risks imposing Western assumptions onto non-Western cultures. Many researchers argue the ideal is a combined approach.

IB Exam Relevance

QuestionBest studies to use

“Explain the use of one research method in one study of cultural influences”Kearins (1981): quasi-experiment with matched samples and four conditions
”Discuss one cultural dimension with reference to one study”Hofstede (2001) framework + Berry (1967) as supporting evidence

”Evaluate an etic approach to studying culture”Hofstede (2001); standardized, replicable, but risks ecological fallacy
”Evaluate an emic approach to studying culture”Bolton (2001); rich insight, not generalizable

Key Concept - Culture Shapes Cognition: The most important insight from this topic is that cognitive processes once assumed to be universal, including memory strategies, conformity tendencies, and emotional responses to events, vary systematically across cultures. This is not because different cultures produce different types of people, but because culture shapes what skills are valued, practiced, and ecologically necessary. Kearins (1981) and Kulkofsky et al. (2011) together make this case across two different cognitive processes: spatial memory and flashbulb memory.
