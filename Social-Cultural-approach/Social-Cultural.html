Behaviour studied include: Attitudes, Attraction, Aggression and Group Dynamics.

Conformity

PSY Social Cultural, Abrams et al (1990) - Asch paradigm

  • Informational conformity
  • Normative
  • Referential conformity

Normative conformity stems from a desire for social acceptance, avoiding punishment, or gaining approval, while informational conformity is driven by the belief that others possess correct information, especially in ambiguous situations. Referential conformity is a broader concept where individuals conform to the standards and expectations of a specific reference group they identify with and aspire to join, which can encompass both normative and informational aspects of that group.

Note

Conformity refers to individuals changing their attitudes or behaviour as a result of real or imagined social pressures. Conformity is a result of social comparison and indirect social influence.

Compliance

Summary

Refers to when individuals change their behaviour publicly to fit in a group, while maintaining personal beliefs in private.

Identification

Summary

Refers to when individuals conform to a group behaviour due to the desire of being associated with them.

Internalization

Summary

Refers to when individuals adopt a group’s beliefs/behaviour both publicly and privately because they align with personal values.

Note

Social Identity Theory argues that a person has not just one “personal self”, but rather several social selves that correspond to a group membership. Humans have a need to know their role/value in social contexts. Hence, tend to categorize ourselves in terms of group membership.

Theory predicts that when one of our Social identities becomes salient; it will influence our behaviour

  • Group memberships give rise to social identities and comparison with other groups, this might lead to bias in information processing like stereotyping and discrimination

  1. Social categorization (in-groups and out-groups)
  2. Social identification (“I’m part of this or that” positive effect in self esteem)
  3. Social comparison (Positive distinctive ness)

  2. Self & Social Categorization
  3. Social Comparison
  4. Humans seek for Positive distinctiveness (In-group and Out-group) (maximize difference / discrimination) with tendency to a concept of in-group superiority or preference.

Heuristics - Schema Theory

PSY cognitive Memory, Thinking and Decision Making, Reconstructive Memory

This note covers two closely related cognitive concepts: Schema Theory and Heuristics. Both explain how the brain processes information efficiently, and both have implications for memory, decision-making, and the reliability of cognitive processes.

Schema Theory

What is a Schema?

A schema is a mental representation of knowledge derived from prior experience, stored in the brain to help organize information, guide behaviour, assist recall, and make sense of new experiences.

Schemas are not passive storage systems. Humans are active information processors: the brain constantly integrates new incoming information (bottom-up, from the senses) with existing knowledge (top-down, from schemas). When information is missing, the brain fills in the gaps based on what schemas predict should be there, which frequently leads to distortions and errors.

Key functions of schemas:

  • Speed up perception, memory, and decision-making
  • Help predict what to expect based on past experience
  • Allow the brain to handle complexity without processing everything from scratch

Types of Schemas

TypeDescriptionExample
Social schemasKnowledge about the world, groups, and social situationsExpectations about how a doctor’s appointment will go
Self-schemasKnowledge and beliefs about oneself”I am good at maths”
ScriptsLearned sequences of behaviour appropriate to specific situationsKnowing how to behave in a restaurant without being told

Scripts are particularly important: they are patterns of behaviour learned through interaction with the environment and are developed within a cultural context, meaning they are not universal across societies.

How Schemas are Created and Updated

ProcessWhat happensWhen it occurs
AssimilationNew information is absorbed into an existing schema without changing itWhen new information fits well with what we already know
AccommodationA new schema is created or an existing one is significantly restructuredWhen new information cannot fit into any existing schema

Schemas and Memory

Schemas have a direct influence on what we remember and how accurately we remember it. Because memory is reconstructive (Bartlett, 1932), schemas act as a scaffold during recall: we remember the gist and fill in the rest based on what our schema tells us should have been there.

This produces predictable distortions:

  • Schema-congruent information (fitting the schema) is more likely to be remembered, but also more likely to be falsely recalled when it was not actually present.
  • Schema-incongruent information (not fitting the schema) is more likely to be forgotten or distorted to fit.
  • Schemas can lead to over-generalisations, including stereotypes, when assumptions based on prior experience are applied too broadly to new people or situations.

Linked studies:

  • Bartlett (1932) - War of Ghosts: Cultural schemas led British participants to distort an unfamiliar Native American folk tale through levelling, sharpening, and rationalisation.
  • Brewer & Treyers (1981): Participants recalled schema-congruent objects from an office as present even when they were not, while schema-incongruent objects were more likely to be forgotten or distorted.
  • Loftus & Palmer (1974) - Car collision: Post-event information (the verb used in a question) activated a schema of a more severe accident, leading to false memory of broken glass.

Heuristics

What is a Heuristic?

A heuristic is a mental shortcut: a simple rule or strategy that focuses on one aspect of a complex problem and ignores others, allowing faster judgements with less cognitive effort.

Heuristics are a type of schema-based processing. Rather than analysing all available information systematically (System 2 thinking), the brain defaults to fast, pattern-matching shortcuts (System 1 thinking).

Why We Use Heuristics: Cognitive Miserliness

Humans are cognitive misers: we tend to avoid deep processing when it is not strictly necessary, in order to conserve cognitive resources. This tendency arises from three factors:

  • Knowledge: we already have schemas that can provide a quick answer
  • Motivation: we often lack the desire to invest effort in a thorough analysis
  • Economy: deep processing is slow and cognitively expensive

The result is a trade-off: heuristics allow faster, more efficient thinking, but at the cost of accuracy and susceptibility to systematic errors (cognitive biases).

Key Heuristics

Anchoring bias (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974): the tendency to rely too heavily on the first piece of numerical information encountered (the anchor) when making subsequent judgements, even when the anchor is arbitrary or irrelevant.

Representativeness heuristic (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983): judging the probability of something by how closely it resembles a prototype or stereotype, ignoring statistical base rates. Produces the conjunction fallacy.

Availability heuristic: estimating the likelihood of an event based on how easily examples come to mind, which tends to overweight dramatic or recent events.

Illusory correlation (Hamilton & Gifford, 1976): perceiving a relationship between two variables (e.g., minority group membership and negative behaviour) when no such relationship exists, because both are distinctive and thus cognitively salient.

Mnemonics

Mnemonics are a positive application of heuristic processing: they use schemas and associations to encode information more efficiently.

Common techniques include:

  • Music, rhythm, and rhyme: exploiting the phonological loop and pattern recognition
  • Acronyms and chunking: grouping information into fewer, more memorable units (cf. Miller’s Magic Number 7)
  • Method of loci: associating information with specific locations in a mentally visualised space (e.g., the “memory palace” technique)

Key Concept - The Overlap between Schemas and Heuristics: Both schemas and heuristics reflect the same underlying principle: the brain prioritises speed and efficiency over accuracy. Schemas provide ready-made frameworks for interpreting new information; heuristics provide ready-made rules for making decisions. Both are highly adaptive in most everyday situations, but both produce predictable, systematic errors when the shortcuts do not match the demands of the task. This is why the study of cognitive biases is, at its core, the study of what happens when efficient mental shortcuts go wrong.

SCLT

PSY Sociocultural Bandura (1961), (1963) & (1965), Joy, Kimball & Zabrack (1986) - Notel Natural Experiment, Enculturation - Acquiring your cultural norms + Acculturation

Social Cognitive Learning Theory (SCLT) was proposed by Albert Bandura as an alternative to behaviourist accounts of learning. Where behaviourism argued that behaviour is shaped only by direct reward and punishment, Bandura argued that humans learn by observing others, retaining what they observe, and then choosing whether to imitate it. This makes SCLT both social (learning happens through watching people in a social environment) and cognitive (mental processes mediate between observation and behaviour).

Core claim: Behaviour can be learned vicariously, by observing a model being rewarded or punished for it, without the observer ever performing the behaviour themselves.

Key Concepts

The Model

A model is any person whose behaviour is observed and potentially imitated. Models do not need to be present in real life; they can be observed through media, film, or television. Factors that increase the likelihood of imitation:

  • Authority: models perceived as having expertise or power are more likely to be imitated
  • Attractiveness: physically or socially attractive models attract more attention
  • Similarity: observers are more likely to imitate models they perceive as similar to themselves (same sex, age, culture, in-group)
  • Liking: warm and friendly models are more likely to be imitated than cold, uncaring ones
  • Consistency: a model who behaves consistently across situations is more likely to be imitated than one whose behaviour varies

The Four Processes of Observational Learning

For imitation to occur, four internal processes must take place. The mnemonic ARMP (Attention, Retention, Motivation, Potential) covers all four.

ProcessDescription
AttentionThe observer must notice and attend to the model’s behaviour. Influenced by the model’s attractiveness, authority, and the desirability of the behaviour.
RetentionThe observed behaviour must be encoded into memory and stored for later retrieval. Cognitive schemas help organise and retain what has been observed.
MotivationThe observer must want to reproduce the behaviour. This depends on outcome expectancy: what reward or punishment the observer anticipates from performing the behaviour.
Potential (Reproduction)The observer must be physically and mentally capable of performing the behaviour.

Vicarious Reinforcement

Vicarious reinforcement occurs when an observer sees a model being rewarded or punished for a behaviour, and adjusts their own likelihood of performing that behaviour accordingly, without ever having performed it themselves.

  • Seeing a model rewarded increases the likelihood of imitation.
  • Seeing a model punished decreases it.
  • This explains Bandura’s (1965) finding: children who saw the model punished showed less imitation, but when offered a reward themselves, they demonstrated they had learned the behaviour regardless — establishing the distinction between acquisition and performance.

Self-Efficacy

Self-efficacy is an individual’s belief in their own ability to successfully perform a behaviour. It is a cognitive factor that determines whether learned behaviours are translated into action.

  • High self-efficacy: the observer believes they can do what the model did, making imitation more likely.
  • Low self-efficacy: the observer doubts their ability, making imitation less likely even if attention, retention, and motivation are present.
  • Individuals with high self-efficacy recover more quickly from setbacks and persist longer in the face of difficulty.

Reciprocal Determinism

Bandura argued that behaviour, the person (cognitive factors), and the environment mutually influence each other in a continuous, dynamic relationship. This is called reciprocal determinism and distinguishes SCLT from simple stimulus-response behaviourism.

How SCLT Explains Enculturation

SCLT is one of the primary mechanisms through which enculturation occurs:

  • Children observe cultural models (parents, peers, media) performing culturally valued behaviours
  • Vicarious reinforcement communicates which behaviours are rewarded and which are punished within the culture
  • Self-efficacy develops as children successfully imitate behaviours
  • Over time, observed cultural norms become internalised standards for behaviour

Fagot (1978) shows this operating unconsciously: parents reinforce gender-appropriate behaviour through praise and criticism without being aware they are doing so. Odden & Rochat (2004) shows it operating without any direct instruction: Samoan children learn fishing and social hierarchy entirely through observation.

IB Exam Relevance

SAQ questions:

  • “Explain Social Cognitive Theory with reference to one study” - Use Bandura (1961); define SCLT, describe the study in APPR format, link findings back to the components of SCLT.
  • “Explain one study of enculturation” - Use Fagot (1978) or Odden & Rochat (2004); Bandura is of marginal relevance for enculturation SAQs because the Bobo doll study shows general learning, not the learning of a cultural norm specifically.

ERQ questions:

  • “Discuss/Evaluate Social Cognitive Theory” - Use Bandura as primary study, Joy et al. and Charlton et al. as supporting/contrasting evidence. Structure: define SCLT and its components, evaluate Bandura (strengths and limitations), use Joy et al. as naturalistic support, use Charlton et al. to show the role of identification and self-efficacy in moderating the effect.
  • “Discuss one or more studies of enculturation of one behaviour” - Use Fagot (1978) and Joy et al. (1986); link explicitly to the SCLT mechanisms involved.

Key distinction for exams: SCLT has stronger explanatory power than predictive power. It can explain why imitation occurs after the fact (what conditions were present) but cannot reliably predict in advance which individuals will imitate and which will not. This is a strong holistic critical thinking point for ERQs.

Key Concept - Acquisition vs. Performance (Bandura, 1965): The most important theoretical refinement in SCLT is the distinction between learning a behaviour and performing it. Children who saw a model punished did not perform the aggressive behaviour, suggesting they had not learned it. But when offered a reward, they demonstrated the behaviour perfectly, showing they had learned it all along. This tells us that vicarious reinforcement does not determine what is learned, only whether it is performed. Exposure to media violence, for example, may teach aggressive scripts even when children do not immediately act on them.
