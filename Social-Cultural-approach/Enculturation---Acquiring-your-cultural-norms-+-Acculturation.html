Enculturation - Acquiring your cultural norms + Acculturation

May 04, 202611 min read

  • PSY
  • Sociocultural

PSY Sociocultural Cultural influences on behaviour + cognition, SCLT, Fagot (1978)

Enculturation is the lifelong process by which individuals learn and internalize the values, beliefs, norms, and behaviours of their own culture. Babies are not born with a culture; they develop one through interaction with gatekeepers such as parents, school, and the media, as well as through peers. Enculturation is not complete at any particular age — it is a continuous process that reinforces identity as a member of a culture.

Key Terms

TermDefinition
EnculturationThe process, beginning in early childhood, by which cultural values, beliefs, and behavioural patterns are adopted by members of a society
Cultural normsShared societal expectations that define what behaviour is appropriate, acceptable, or inappropriate within a culture
Direct tuitionTeaching children how to behave through reinforcing appropriate behaviour and discouraging inappropriate behaviour, either through praise or punishment
Participatory learningChildren engage actively in a cultural activity and transfer that learning to later situations, without explicit instruction
Vertical transmissionThe passing of cultural norms downward from one generation to the next (e.g., parent to child)
Horizontal transmissionThe transfer of cultural norms among peers of the same generation
ChannellingAdults indirectly guiding children toward culturally appropriate behaviour by structuring their environment and reinforcing certain activities
Values enculturationThe enculturation of cognition: attitudes about social relationships, gender roles, time orientation, and beliefs about health, illness, and morality

How Enculturation Occurs

Enculturation does not happen through one mechanism alone. The PDF source identifies three main routes:

1. Direct tuition: Parents, teachers, or other gatekeepers explicitly tell children what to do and what not to do. Rewards reinforce culturally appropriate behaviour; punishments discourage inappropriate behaviour. This is the most visible form of enculturation.

2. Social Cognitive Theory (observational learning): Children learn by observing models in their culture and imitating behaviours that are reinforced. Vicarious reinforcement, seeing others rewarded for a behaviour, increases the likelihood of imitation. This operates without explicit instruction and often without awareness.

3. Participatory learning: Children engage in cultural activities alongside others, learning through doing rather than being told. Odden & Rochat (2004) demonstrated this in Samoa: children learned line fishing and understanding of the social hierarchy of the chief system entirely through observation and self-directed experimentation, with no adult instruction.

Enculturation and Gender Roles

One of the most studied examples of enculturation is the development of gender roles: socially constructed patterns of behaviour, attitudes, and expectations that a culture considers appropriate for boys and girls.

Gender roles are not innate; they are enculturated through:

  • Direct tuition: Parents explicitly encourage gender-appropriate play and discourage gender-inappropriate behaviour
  • Channelling: Adults structure children’s environments (e.g., toy selection) in gender-typed ways without necessarily being aware of it
  • Observational learning: Children observe and imitate gendered behaviour modelled by parents and peers

Key Studies

Fagot (1978) - Parental Reinforcement of Gender Roles

Method: Overt naturalistic observation of 24 families (12 with a boy, 12 with a girl). Children aged 20-24 months. Observed across five 60-minute sessions over five weeks using a checklist of 46 child behaviours and 19 parental reactions. Time sampling every 60 seconds. Two observers used to establish inter-coder reliability (0.93 for child behaviour, 0.83 for parental reaction). Parents also completed a questionnaire on sex-role socialisation after observations.

Findings: Parents reacted significantly more favourably to gender-appropriate behaviour and more negatively to gender-inappropriate behaviour. In a follow-up interview, parents’ self-perceptions did not match what was observed, indicating that gender socialisation is largely unconscious.

Link to enculturation: Demonstrates direct tuition and channelling: parents are transmitting culturally defined gender norms to children through reward and punishment, without being aware they are doing so.

Odden & Rochat (2004) - Observational Learning in Samoa

Method: Longitudinal study of 25 months on 28 children in a single Samoan village. Used both observations and interviews.

Background: Samoan culture has a very high power distance index. Social norms limit adult-child interaction time. Parents take a non-interventionist approach; children are largely left to learn on their own.

Findings: Young male children observed adult males fishing without any direct instruction. Around age 10, children would borrow adult fishing equipment and experiment independently. By age 12, most could fish alone. A multiple-choice test on the chief hierarchy system given to 46 twelve-year-olds found the majority had a broad understanding of the social rituals of their society, despite receiving no formal instruction on it until high school.

Link to enculturation: Demonstrates participatory learning and observational learning as routes to enculturation. Cultural norms, including both skills and social hierarchies, are acquired through active observation rather than direct teaching.

Kearins (1981) - Culture and Spatial Cognition

Aboriginal Australian children significantly outperformed white Australian children on visual-spatial memory tasks across all four conditions, despite object familiarity. This demonstrates values enculturation: the desert ecological environment, transmitted through parenting practices, language (cardinal directions), and songlines, shapes cognitive strategies across generations.

Link to enculturation: Cultural transmission of spatial skills through participatory learning (wayfinding from a young age), language structure (cardinal directions), and community practices (songlines) produces measurably different cognitive abilities.

StrengthsLimitations
Takes into account the social and cultural context in which enculturation occursCannot explain variation within cultures: why some individuals conform to gender norms more than others (e.g., non-conformists)
Predicts that children acquire internal standards through reward/punishment, by personal or vicarious experience: supported by Fagot (1978)Since the majority of caregivers are women, boys and girls have very similar early experiences. SCT would predict more feminine gender identity across both sexes, which is not observed
Multiple empirical studies support the notion of modelling (Bandura, Fagot, Odden & Rochat)Enculturation involves too many variables to be reduced to observational learning alone; direct tuition, participatory learning, and peers all contribute

SAQ Guidance: “Explain one study of enculturation”

The marking PDFs identify the following as required for full marks:

  1. Define enculturation clearly and concisely
  2. Choose a study that directly demonstrates the process or effects of enculturation (not just general learning)
  3. Describe the study accurately: aim, participants, procedure, findings
  4. Explicitly link the study back to enculturation, explaining what it demonstrates about how cultural norms are transmitted

Common mistakes (flagged in the marking PDFs):

  • Using Bandura’s Bobo Doll as the enculturation study. It shows how behaviour may be learned but does not directly show how a cultural norm is transmitted. It is of marginal relevance and scores only 2 marks.
  • Failing to define enculturation
  • Using an acculturation study instead of an enculturation study
  • Describing the study accurately but failing to link it back to the question

Best study for this SAQ: Fagot (1978). Score 7+ by: defining enculturation (including direct tuition), describing Fagot’s 24 families, naturalistic observation checklist, finding that parents reinforced gender-appropriate behaviour unconsciously, and explicitly linking this to enculturation as the process by which cultural gender norms are transmitted through reward and punishment.

Acculturation

Acculturation (Berry, 2004) is the process of cultural and psychological change that takes place as a result of contact between two or more cultural groups. At the individual level, it involves changes in a person’s behaviour, values, and identity. It differs from enculturation in a critical way: enculturation is acquiring the norms of your own culture from birth; acculturation is adapting to a different culture through sustained contact.

People may enter a new culture either voluntarily (migrant workers, expatriates, international students) or involuntarily (refugees, asylum seekers). The nature of this entry significantly shapes the acculturation experience.

Berry’s Four Acculturation Strategies (1974)

Berry proposed that individuals navigate acculturation along two dimensions: how much they value maintaining their original culture, and how much they seek a positive relationship with the new one.

Positive relationship with new culture: YESPositive relationship with new culture: NO
Maintain original culture: YESIntegration (bicultural)Separation
Maintain original culture: NOAssimilationMarginalisation
  • Integration: The individual maintains their original cultural identity while also engaging with and adapting to the new culture. The research evidence consistently shows this produces the best psychological outcomes.
  • Assimilation: The individual abandons their original culture and fully adopts the new one.
  • Separation: The individual maintains their original culture and minimises contact with the new culture.
  • Marginalisation: The individual cannot maintain the original culture (e.g., due to displacement or trauma) but is also excluded from the new one by discrimination. Associated with the worst psychological outcomes.

Berry argues the strategy chosen reflects both individual choice and the degree to which the host culture is welcoming or exclusionary.

Acculturative Stress

The tension between maintaining one’s original identity and adapting to a new culture often produces acculturative stress: the psychological, somatic, and social difficulties that accompany acculturation. It manifests as anxiety, depression, social difficulties, and physical symptoms. It is sometimes called culture shock.

A related concept is the acculturation gap: generational differences in the speed of acculturation within immigrant families. Children acculturate faster than parents through school and peer contact, creating tension when they live by different cultural expectations at home versus outside it.

Reactive identification: When discrimination is experienced, some individuals respond by strengthening their original cultural identity rather than integrating. This can be negative (hardening of oppositional attitudes) or positive (as seen in the Civil Rights movement, where a strong African-American identity became a source of empowerment).

Key Study: Lueck & Wilson (2010)

A nationally representative semi-structured interview study of 2,095 Asian Americans (including Chinese, Filipino, and Vietnamese). Interviewers were culturally and linguistically matched to participants. A randomly selected subsample was re-contacted to validate interview data.

70% of participants showed acculturative stress. Key predictors:

FactorEffect on acculturative stress
Bilingual language preferenceSignificantly reduces stress: builds support networks in both communities
English-only preferencePredictor of higher stress: cuts off access to heritage community support
Perceived discriminationStrongest predictor of high stress
Family cohesion (shared values, closeness)Significantly reduces stress
Economic satisfactionLower stress among those satisfied with opportunities in the US
Would migrate againLower stress among those who would make the same choice

Core finding: Integration, maintaining bilingualism and cultural ties while adapting to the new culture, is significantly more protective than assimilation. Simply learning English and abandoning the heritage culture increases, not decreases, stress.

The Immigrant Paradox

Research reveals a counterintuitive pattern: greater acculturation into the host culture is sometimes associated with worse health outcomes.

  • Alegria et al. (2007): Hispanics born in or long-settled in the US were more likely to be diagnosed with psychiatric disorders than recent arrivals.
  • Allen et al. (2008): Hispanic adolescents who speak mostly Spanish are less likely to use drugs and alcohol.

However, these studies are unidimensional: they only measure the relationship with either the host or heritage culture, not both. Wang et al. (2010), using a bidimensional approach with 119 Cuban-American students in Miami, found that biculturalism (high engagement with both cultures) was associated with higher self-esteem, lower depression, and lower anxiety. This suggests the paradox disappears when both cultural relationships are measured simultaneously.

Limitations of Berry’s Model

  • Focuses on the individual’s behaviour, not the behaviour or policies of the host culture.
  • Assumes four discrete categories; in practice, acculturation strategies may shift depending on social context (LaFromboise’s Alternation Model, 1993: people move fluidly between cultures depending on situation).
  • Implies a hierarchical relationship between cultures.
  • Too simplistic: does not account for reason for migration, cultural distance between home and host cultures, age of migration, trauma history, or socioeconomic status.

Methodological Challenges in Acculturation Research

  • Language: tests may be linguistically inaccessible or culturally interpreted differently
  • Representative samples: many variables influence the acculturation experience, making generalization difficult
  • Attrition: historically high dropout rates in longitudinal acculturation studies
  • Ethics: undocumented participants require strong anonymity protections; power dynamics between researcher and immigrant participant must be carefully managed; participants may not be familiar with their rights

The APA Presidential Task Force on Immigration (2012) summarized the challenge as: “Who is given tests in what language by whom, when, and where?”
