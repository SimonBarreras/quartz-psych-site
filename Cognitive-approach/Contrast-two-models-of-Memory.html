Working Memory Model, Multi-Store Model of Memory (MSM)

Models are used to simplify this process; to understand and predict behaviour; to educate and structure research.

Two models of memory are MSM & WMM

HM (Milner, 1996)

Localization of function, Biological Study

  • Milner conducted a Longitudinal Case Study on Henry Molaison (HM), one of the most studied individuals in the history of neuroscience, whose specific pattern of memory loss following brain surgery provided landmark evidence for the localization of memory function and the role of the hippocampus.

Background:

  • Henry Molaison suffered a cycling accident at age 7, leading to severe epilepsy from age 10.
  • By age 27, seizures had become so debilitating that they prevented a normal life.
  • With family consent, HM underwent experimental surgery in which sections of the medial temporal lobe (including most of the hippocampus, amygdala, and entorhinal cortex) were bilaterally removed.
  • The surgery successfully reduced seizures, but produced unexpected and profound memory changes.

Aim: To investigate and document the nature of HM’s memory impairment following bilateral hippocampal removal, and what this reveals about the role of the hippocampus in memory.

Method: Case study using method triangulation:

  • Psychometric testing (IQ testing; HM’s IQ remained in the normal range)
  • Direct behavioural observation
  • Structured interviews
  • Cognitive memory testing (recall, recognition, learning tasks)
  • MRI scanning by Corkin (1997) to confirm extent of damage

Results - HM’s Memory Profile:

Memory TypeIntact?Details
Long-term memories (pre-surgery)IntactChildhood and pre-surgery memories were largely preserved
Procedural memoryIntactCould learn new motor skills (e.g., mirror drawing task), improving with practice even without remembering doing it
Short-term memory (working memory)IntactCould hold information briefly in mind
New episodic memoryImpairedCould not form new memories of events (anterograde amnesia)
New semantic memoryImpairedCould not learn new factual information

Key Insight: HM could improve on motor tasks through practice, showing procedural memory is preserved, but would have no recollection of having practised, demonstrating a clear dissociation between explicit (declarative) and implicit (procedural) memory systems.

Conclusion: The hippocampus is not the site of permanent memory storage but is essential for consolidating and organizing new explicit memories (episodic and semantic). Without it, new declarative memories cannot be formed, but procedural learning remains intact.

Evaluation

StrengthsLimitations
Method triangulation (psychometric, observational, interview, MRI) increases validity and reliabilitySingle case study: HM’s brain damage was unique; cannot generalize directly
Provided the first clear evidence for separate memory systems (explicit vs. implicit)HM’s surgery removed multiple structures, making it impossible to isolate the hippocampus alone
Longitudinal: HM was studied for over 40 years, providing rich, detailed dataEthical concerns: HM could not consent to ongoing research due to his amnesia (family consented)

Key Concept - Anterograde Amnesia: Anterograde amnesia is the inability to form new long-term memories after a brain injury, while old memories remain intact. HM’s case proved that the hippocampus is essential for the consolidation of new explicit (declarative) memories. It also demonstrated that procedural memory (implicit) is stored elsewhere, likely in the basal ganglia and cerebellum, leading to the modern understanding of multiple memory systems.

Glanzer and Cunitz (1966)

Study cognitive Multi-Store Model of Memory (MSM)

Glanzer and Cunitz conducted two related Lab Experiments providing strong empirical support for the Multi-Store Model of Memory, demonstrating that the primacy and recency effects reflect two distinct memory stores (LTM and STM respectively), and that the recency effect can be selectively eliminated by an interference task while the primacy effect remains intact.

Background: Atkinson and Shiffrin’s Multi-Store Model proposes that STM and LTM are separate stores. The serial position effect is a key prediction of this model: words at the beginning of a list (primacy) are rehearsed into LTM, while words at the end (recency) are still held in STM. Words in the middle are displaced from STM before being consolidated into LTM.

Aim: To investigate whether manipulating rehearsal time (Study 1) enhances the primacy effect, and whether an interference task (Study 2) selectively eliminates the recency effect, supporting the existence of separate STM and LTM stores.

Study 1 - Primacy Effect

Participants: 240 Army enlisted men, randomly allocated to six conditions (40 per condition) using an independent samples design.

Method:

  • Participants listened to recordings of eight 20-word lists of common one-syllable words, preceded by two 5-word practice lists.
  • The six conditions varied the presentation rate and repetition of each word:
ConditionRate (seconds)Repetition
13Once
23Twice in succession
36Once
46Twice in succession
59Once
69Twice in succession
  • After each list, participants had 2 minutes to write down as many words as they could recall in any order.

Results:

  • Increased time intervals between words led to greater recall of words across the list, but not for words at the end of the list (recency effect was unchanged).
  • The effect of repetition on recall was significant only in the 3-second rate condition; there was no significant difference between 6 or 9-second rates.
  • This supports the view that the primacy effect is the result of rehearsal: more time between words allowed greater acoustic rehearsal and consolidation into LTM.

Study 2 - Recency Effect

Participants: 46 Army enlisted men using a repeated measures design.

Method:

  • Participants were shown 15 fifteen-word lists of common one-syllable words on a screen, preceded by three 5-word practice lists.
  • Each word was shown for 1 second with a 2-second interval between words; the experimenter read each word aloud as it appeared.
  • At the end of each list, participants saw either a # or a number between 0 and 9: - # sign: write immediately (immediate recall) - Number: count aloud from that number until told to stop, then write (interference task)

The

| Condition | Delay | | ---------------- | ---------------------- | | Immediate recall | 0 seconds | | Delayed recall | 10 seconds of counting | | Delayed recall | 30 seconds of counting |

Results:

  • Immediate recall: both primacy and recency effects were observed (classic U-shaped serial position curve).
  • 10-second delay: significant reduction in the recency effect.
  • 30-second delay: researchers reported “no trace” of the recency effect; primacy effect remained fully intact.

Conclusion: The selective elimination of the recency effect (but not the primacy effect) by a distractor task demonstrates that the two effects reflect two separate memory systems. The recency effect depends on STM: distraction displaces the most recently heard words before they can be recalled. The primacy effect depends on LTM: those words were already consolidated through rehearsal and are unaffected by the distractor. This provides strong empirical support for the MSM’s claim that STM and LTM are distinct stores.

Evaluation

StrengthsLimitations
Two complementary studies attack the same question from both sides (primacy and recency), producing a more complete pictureLow ecological validity: word list memorisation under timed conditions does not reflect real-world memory
Selective elimination of recency (not primacy) is a precise, theoretically meaningful findingSample: Army enlisted men only; not representative of the general population
Study 1 uses independent samples (controls for practice effects); Study 2 uses repeated measures (reduces individual differences)Participant attrition from Study 1 to Study 2 (240 to 46) raises questions about comparability
Words were common one-syllable words, controlling for familiarity and length as confoundsResults may reflect encoding strategies rather than truly separate stores; depth of processing (Craik & Lockhart) offers an alternative explanation
Results are among the most replicated findings in memory researchThe MSM’s concept of STM as a single passive store is challenged by Baddeley & Hitch’s Working Memory Model

Key Concept - Serial Position Effect: The serial position curve shows that recall is highest for words at the beginning (primacy) and end (recency) of a list, and lowest for words in the middle. Glanzer & Cunitz (1966) demonstrated that the recency effect is fragile: a brief distractor task eliminates it entirely. The primacy effect, by contrast, survives because those words were already rehearsed into LTM. This double dissociation is one of the clearest pieces of evidence that STM and LTM are genuinely separate stores.

    Warrington and Shallice (1970) - KF

    Study Biological cognitive Working Memory Model, Multi-Store Model of Memory (MSM)

    Warrington and Shallice conducted a Case Study on patient KF, who suffered brain damage after a motorcycle accident. KF’s pattern of memory impairment provided crucial evidence for both the Multi-Store Model and the Working Memory Model.

    Aim: To investigate the nature of KF’s memory impairment following brain damage, and what his specific pattern of deficits reveals about the structure of memory.

    Participant: Patient KF, a single male patient who sustained damage to the left parietal-occipital region of the brain in a motorcycle accident.

    Method: Case study using multiple cognitive assessments.

    • KF was given a battery of psychometric tests assessing STM and LTM.
    • Verbal and visual memory were tested separately.
    • Tasks included digit span, free recall, and recognition tests.

    Results:

    • KF’s LTM was largely intact; he could recall events from before the accident normally.
    • KF’s verbal STM was severely impaired; his digit span was only 1-2 items (normal = 7 +/- 2).
    • Visual STM was relatively unaffected; KF could recall visual information normally.
    • KF could still learn new long-term information despite impaired verbal STM, contradicting the MSM’s assumption that LTM requires STM rehearsal.

    Conclusion:

    • KF’s dissociation of verbal STM from intact LTM challenges the MSM’s linear flow (sensory → STM → LTM).
    • The separation of verbal and visual STM supports the Working Memory Model (phonological loop vs. visuospatial sketchpad as distinct components).
    • KF forms a double dissociation with HM (HM: intact STM, damaged LTM transfer; KF: damaged verbal STM, intact LTM transfer).

    Evaluation

    StrengthsLimitations
    Real neurological damage provides ecologically valid evidence about memory structureSingle case study: cannot generalize to broader population
    Creates a double dissociation with HM, strengthening evidence for separate STM/LTMCannot determine exact cause of impairment (brain damage affects multiple systems)
    Supports separate verbal and visual STM components (WMM)No control group; impossible to make causal inferences
    Directly challenges the MSM’s strict serial flow modelEthical concerns around repeated testing of a brain-injured patient
    Method triangulation (multiple cognitive tests) increases reliabilityKF’s injury may have caused other unrelated cognitive changes

    • Paired with: HM case study, Atkinson & Shiffrin (1968), Baddeley & Hitch (1974)

    Key Concept - Double Dissociation (HM vs. KF): HM had intact STM but could not transfer to LTM. KF had damaged verbal STM but intact LTM. Together, these two cases form a double dissociation, proving that STM and LTM are genuinely separate systems. KF also supports the WMM by showing that verbal and visual STM are distinct components, consistent with the phonological loop and visuospatial sketchpad.

Landry and Bartling (2011)

Study cognitive Working Memory Model, Articulatory Suppression

Landry and Bartling conducted a Lab Experiment testing the Working Memory Model by using articulatory suppression to disrupt the phonological loop, demonstrating that this component plays a specific and necessary role in verbal serial recall.

Aim: To investigate whether articulatory suppression (a concurrent verbal task) impairs the serial recall of a written list of phonologically dissimilar letters, as predicted by the phonological loop component of Baddeley & Hitch’s Working Memory Model.

Participants: 34 undergraduate psychology students, randomly assigned to two independent groups.

Method: Lab experiment with independent groups design.

GroupTaskArticulatory suppression?
ControlMemorize letter listNo - silent memorization
ExperimentalMemorize letter listYes - repeatedly say “1, 2” at 2 per second

Stimuli: 10 lists of 7 letters each, drawn from F, K, L, M, R, X, Q, chosen because they are phonologically dissimilar (do not sound alike), to control for phonological similarity effects.

Procedure:

  • Each list was displayed for 5 seconds, followed by a 5-second wait, then written recall on an answer sheet.
  • Recall was scored as correct only if the letter was in the correct serial position.
  • Average percentage correct recall was calculated for each participant.

Results:

GroupMean % Correct RecallSD
Control76%0.13
Experimental (articulatory suppression)45%0.14
  • A t-test confirmed the difference was statistically significant at p < 0.01.
  • Standard deviations were nearly identical across groups, indicating consistent within-group performance.

Conclusion: Articulatory suppression significantly impaired serial recall of verbal material. Because the suppression task (saying “1, 2”) occupied the phonological loop’s articulatory rehearsal process, participants in the experimental group could not rehearse the letter strings, leading to substantially lower accuracy. This directly supports the WMM’s prediction that the phonological loop is essential for verbal working memory.

Evaluation

StrengthsLimitations
Controlled experiment with random assignment; high internal validityArtificial task: memorizing random letter strings has low ecological validity
Clear operationalization of articulatory suppression as the IVSmall sample (34 students) limits generalizability
Statistical analysis (t-test, SD) adds quantitative rigourParticipants were all psychology undergraduates; potential demand characteristics
Directly and specifically tests the phonological loop component of the WMMOnly tested one component of WMM; does not address central executive or visuospatial sketchpad
Results align precisely with Baddeley & Hitch’s (1974) theoretical predictionsTask may not represent how phonological loop functions in real-world multitasking

Key Concept - Articulatory Suppression: Articulatory suppression is a technique where participants are made to repeat an irrelevant sound (e.g., “1, 2, 1, 2”) continuously. This occupies the articulatory rehearsal process of the phonological loop, preventing it from being used to rehearse other verbal material. Landry & Bartling (2011) uses this to isolate the phonological loop and confirm its specific, necessary role in verbal working memory.
