Emotion in Memory

Emotion in Memory

May 04, 20267 min read

  • PSY
  • cognitive
  • Biological

PSY cognitive Biological Flashbulb Memory, Memory, Hormones In Human Behaviour

Emotions are rich and diverse, and they clearly affect how we remember things. Some memories feel permanent and photographic while others fade quickly. The key variable appears to be emotional significance: we tend to remember better those experiences that involve strong emotion. This topic examines why, and whether emotionally charged memories are truly more accurate or simply feel that way.

The Core Question

Does emotion enhance memory, distort it, or both?

The evidence suggests the answer is: both, depending on context. Emotion reliably enhances the vividness and confidence of a memory. Whether it also enhances accuracy is more contested and depends heavily on the nature of the emotional event and the method used to study it.

Flashbulb Memory Theory (Brown & Kulik, 1977)

Flashbulb Memory (FBM) is the theory that emotionally significant and surprising public events produce an unusually vivid, detailed, and lasting memory of the circumstances in which a person heard the news (not of the event itself).

Brown & Kulik proposed two key conditions for FBM formation:

  1. Surprise (the event is unexpected)
  2. Consequentiality (the event matters personally or nationally)

When both are present, they argued a “Now Print!” neurological mechanism is triggered, essentially photographing the moment in memory.

Criteria for a memory to qualify as a FBM:

  • Personal context (where you were, what you were doing)
  • Emotional impact
  • Vividness and detail
  • Long-lasting
  • Public or shared element
  • Strengthened by repeated rehearsal (retelling the story)

Note: FBM is a theory about the circumstances surrounding a shocking event, not about memory for the event itself.

The Biological Mechanism: Adrenaline and the Amygdala

The “Now Print!” mechanism Brown & Kulik proposed has since been given a neurobiological explanation through the work of McGaugh, Cahill, and Sharot.

How it works:

  1. An emotionally significant event triggers the release of adrenaline (epinephrine) from the adrenal glands.
  2. Adrenaline activates the amygdala (the brain’s emotional processing centre).
  3. The amygdala then modulates the hippocampus, strengthening the encoding of the memory into long-term storage.
  4. The result is a memory that is more vivid, more detailed, and more resistant to forgetting than a neutral memory encoded under normal conditions.

This process is called emotional memory consolidation.

McGaugh & Cahill (1995) provided direct causal evidence for this mechanism. In a double-blind placebo-controlled experiment, participants who received propranolol (a beta-blocker that blocks adrenaline) showed no memory enhancement for emotionally arousing story content, while the placebo group remembered the emotional section significantly better. This isolates adrenaline as the key biological variable.

Sharot et al. (2007) provided neuroimaging support. Using fMRI, they showed that 9/11 memories activated the amygdala more strongly than control memories, and that this activation correlated with the vividness and confidence of the memory. Greater proximity to Ground Zero produced greater amygdala activation, suggesting a dose-response relationship between emotional intensity and biological encoding.

Are Flashbulb Memories Accurate?

This is the central critical debate in this topic. The evidence is genuinely divided.

The case that FBMs are accurate

Yuille & Cutshall (1986) studied 13 witnesses to a real armed robbery in Vancouver. When re-interviewed 4-5 months later, their memories were remarkably accurate compared to original police transcripts. Misleading questions had almost no effect, and witnesses who reported higher stress during the event showed greater accuracy, not less.

This supports the idea that real emotional events produce robust, resistant memories, and challenges the assumption (from lab studies like Loftus & Palmer) that emotion impairs accuracy.

The case that FBMs are not reliably accurate

Neisser & Harsch (1992) tested this directly. The morning after the Challenger disaster, students completed a questionnaire about where they were and what they were doing. 2.5 years later, the same students answered the same questionnaire again. Accuracy was very low (average 4.7/7), with 25% scoring 0-2. Crucially, participants showed high confidence in their inaccurate memories, and several refused to believe their original account when shown it.

This reveals a critical dissociation: emotional significance increases confidence and vividness but does not guarantee accuracy. The memory feels like a photograph but is still reconstructive.

The key variable appears to be personal proximity and direct emotional involvement. Sharot et al. (2007) supports this: only participants near Ground Zero formed strong FBMs. Hearing about an event second-hand, even a shocking one, may not trigger the full adrenaline-amygdala mechanism that produces genuinely enhanced encoding.

Reliability of Flashbulb Memory as a Concept

Brown & Kulik’s original claims have been challenged on several grounds:

  • The study was retrospective: memories were not measured at the time, so there is no baseline for accuracy.
  • It relied on self-report, with no way to verify what participants actually remembered at the time.
  • The “Now Print!” mechanism is theoretically speculative: Brown & Kulik proposed it before neuroimaging evidence existed.
  • It cannot control for rehearsal: FBMs may feel vivid not because of a special encoding mechanism, but because we retell and revisit them frequently.
  • Social desirability: participants may report more confidence and detail to avoid appearing to have forgotten something they “should” remember.

What the evidence collectively supports:

Emotion does reliably influence memory, but the mechanism is more nuanced than a simple on/off “special” system. The amygdala and adrenaline play a genuine biological role (McGaugh & Cahill; Sharot). However, this enhanced encoding does not make memories immune to distortion (Neisser & Harsch). The confidence we feel in FBMs is not a reliable guide to their accuracy.

IB Exam Relevance

SAQ questions this topic answers:

  • Explain one study of the influence of emotion on one cognitive process (Brown & Kulik or McGaugh & Cahill)
  • Explain one ethical consideration in one study of the effect of emotion on cognition (McGaugh & Cahill: drug administration to healthy participants)
  • Explain the use of one research method in one study of the effect of emotion on cognition (McGaugh & Cahill: double-blind experiment; or Sharot: fMRI correlational study)

ERQ questions this topic answers:

  • Discuss/Evaluate the role of emotion on one cognitive process
  • Discuss/Evaluate research into FBM
  • Discuss one theory of how emotion affects one cognitive process

For SAQs: Use McGaugh & Cahill (1995) as your primary study. It is a true double-blind experiment with a clear causal mechanism, directly linking adrenaline to memory enhancement in humans.

For ERQs: Lead with McGaugh & Cahill (1995), evaluate its methodology specifically (double-blind strength, low ecological validity, drug ethics). Then bring in Sharot et al. (2007) for neuroimaging convergent validity. For holistic CT, use Neisser & Harsch (1992) to challenge whether enhanced encoding actually means enhanced accuracy, and raise the rehearsal confound as a limitation of the FBM literature as a whole.
